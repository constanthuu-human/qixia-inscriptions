Teachings · Practices · Networks

Buddhist branches in Nanjing

Nanjing Buddhism was not one uniform belief system. Different teachings and practices answered different questions, used different texts, and gathered different communities.

One connected city

Different Buddhist branches in Nanjing

The labels below are useful entry points, not sealed boxes. Monks and laypeople could participate in more than one devotional world.

The evidence is stronger for some periods than others. The page therefore distinguishes documented lineages from broad practices that crossed institutional boundaries.

Tiantai

How can many teachings fit together?

Tiantai organized Buddhist teachings through classification, scripture study, meditation, and ritual practice. Zhiyi and Guanding are important for understanding how teachings were transmitted and preserved.

Nanjing connection: Sui–Tang regional networks, Qixia and other long-lived monasteries, and the movement of teachers and texts.

Pure Land devotion

How can ordinary people seek rebirth?

Recitation and devotion to Amitabha offered a practice that could be shared by monastics and laypeople. It could operate inside a monastery without requiring every participant to master complex doctrine.

Nanjing connection: urban temples, memorial practice, and lay devotional communities; exact temple assignments should be source-checked.

Chan

How does practice change the mind?

Chan emphasized meditation, teacher–student transmission, and direct cultivation. Its later presence in Nanjing belongs to networks of teachers, mountain monasteries, and urban institutions.

Nanjing connection: Qingliang and other monasteries are useful places to investigate, but do not assume a single unbroken lineage without evidence.

Vinaya

How does a community stay disciplined?

Vinaya traditions organized ordination, conduct, communal rules, and the material life of a monastery. They explain how a temple could function beyond the charisma of one teacher.

Nanjing connection: every monastery depended on discipline, even when it was not identified with one exclusive school.

Ritual and esoteric practice

How can ritual protect a community?

Mantras, rites, images, relics, and protective ceremonies connected temples to healing, protection, merit, and state ritual. These practices often crossed school boundaries.

Nanjing connection: imperial temples and relic landscapes, especially in the Ming, made ritual power publicly visible.

Lay Buddhist practice

How do non-monks participate?

Donating, copying texts, making images, attending ceremonies, visiting relics, and supporting repairs allowed laypeople to shape Buddhist institutions without becoming monks.

Nanjing connection: inscriptions and rebuilding records make donors and local communities visible.

Why these branches developed

Reasons behind the branches

Different practices became influential because Nanjing connected people, institutions, texts, resources, and political power.

Different questions

Different needs

People asked different questions about salvation, discipline, meditation, protection, merit, and the relationship between texts and practice.

Texts and translation

Ideas could travel

Jiankang’s educated court and urban networks created audiences for translated scriptures, commentaries, biographies, and debates.

Patronage

Support shaped institutions

Emperors, officials, aristocratic families, merchants, and ordinary donors supported different temples and ceremonies for different reasons.

Urban and mountain space

Place shaped practice

Urban temples encouraged public contact and teaching; mountain monasteries supported retreat, pilgrimage, images, and distinctive sacred landscapes.

Mobility

Monks carried lineages

Travelling teachers, disciples, translators, and pilgrims connected Nanjing to wider regions. A branch was often a network before it was a fixed label.

Historical change

Institutions were rebuilt

War, relocation, imperial policy, and reconstruction changed which teachings and temples were most visible in each period.

The community

“Buddhism in Nanjing” means more than monks and temples. It includes novices, donors, translators, cooks, artisans, stone carvers, officials, pilgrims, caretakers, and the people who remembered a place after it was rebuilt.

Why Nanjing mattered

Nanjing mattered because it repeatedly connected political authority with transport, learning, urban life, and mountain pilgrimage. Teachings became visible here when people gave them institutions, objects, routes, and shared memory.
