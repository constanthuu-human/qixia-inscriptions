Stories · Memory · Sacred places

Buddhist legends in Nanjing

Legends did more than entertain. They explained why a place mattered, why a patron acted, and why a community should continue to care for a temple.

Stories that made Nanjing sacred

A sacred city is built through remembered events.

Read each story in two ways: as religious meaning and as historical evidence.

A miracle story can reveal what a community valued even when it cannot prove every event literally happened.

Jianye · Relics

Kang Senghui and the relics

A tradition tells of Kang Senghui bringing relic devotion into the Eastern Wu capital. The story presents relics as proof that Buddhism could belong in a new political and cultural world.

Historical use: study how a foreign religion gained public recognition. The exact chronology is not fully secure.

Mount She · Foundation

Ming Sengshao gives his residence

The Qixia foundation story turns a scholar-recluse’s mountain residence into a monastery. It explains sacred space as the result of donation, renunciation, and monastic organization.

Historical use: connect elite retreat culture with the creation of an institution.

Liang court · Reputation

Baizhi and imperial attention

Stories about Baozhi’s unusual religious authority place a charismatic monk beside Emperor Wu’s court. The story makes a relationship between spiritual reputation and political patronage visible.

Historical use: distinguish miracle memory from administrative evidence while studying patronage.

Qixia · Relic landscape

The pagoda and the sacred mountain

Relic traditions associated with Qixia connected a mountain monastery to a wider Buddhist geography. A pagoda made that connection visible and gave pilgrims a focus for ritual memory.

Historical use: follow how relics, buildings, and pilgrimage reinforce one another.

Dabao'en · Imperial memory

The great pagoda of Nanjing

The Ming construction of Dabao'en transformed an older religious zone into an imperial monument. Its scale and glazed architecture expressed patronage, filial rhetoric, and the power to rebuild the past.

Historical use: compare archaeological remains with later reconstructions and public memory.

Qixia · Modern memory

A temple as refuge

Modern accounts remember Qixia as a place where civilians sought protection during wartime. The story makes a monastery sacred through an act of care, not only through ancient foundation.

Historical use: test temple memory against wartime records and testimony.

Religious meaning

Why stories endure

Legends connect a visible place to an invisible order: relics, protection, compassion, miraculous authority, or the continuing presence of a founder.

Historical method

What stories can and cannot prove

They can show how communities understood a site and what they wanted later visitors to remember. They cannot automatically prove an exact date, location, or supernatural event.

A useful habit

Keep the story and the evidence beside each other.

On the Qixia inscription pages, place the narrative next to the text, translation, image, and historical interpretation. That layout lets visitors feel the force of a story without confusing devotional memory with direct proof.
