The Site

A southern landscape of belief and authority.

Carved at Mount She near Jiankang, the Qixia grottoes emerged from Southern Dynasties aristocratic Buddhism. Their inscriptions record devotion, family memory, monastic expansion, and the political use of Buddhist patronage.

The Ming Zhengjun Stele

明征君碑

Chinese Text

朕闻锺山玉阙，羽驾之所巡游；昆岳金台，衣之所翔集。虽复真宗窅眇，神理希微，犹居三界之中，未出九天之外。唯有乘如广运，妙觉圆明，因无生以济有生，就无象而成大象。道隔去来之际，筌系靡得其端，理忘动寂之机，随迎罕窥其奥。得其门者，如髻宝之希逢；臻其极者，似昙花之难遇。

南齐征君明僧绍者，平原人也。仲雍诞其绵允，井伯播其灵苗，芳源肇于孟明，因即以明为姓。曾祖忱，晋著作郎。祖玩，晋建威将军。凤经流誉，雅韵隆于八儒；豹略申威，香名高于七校。父略，宋平原太守中书侍郎。朱明出抚，扬惠化而傍霑；紫诰攸司，罄忠规而奉上。征君早植净因，宿苞种智。悟真空于绮岁，体法性于青襟。照与神通，心将道合。遗荣轩冕，少无尘杂之情；讬志林岩，自叶幽贞之趣。亭亭秀气，掩璧月而架丹霄；皎皎清衿，漱琼湍而凌碧濑。即相非相，指万象为虚空；无我无人，等四流于寂灭。加以学穷儒肆，该综典坟，论极元津，精通《老》、《易》。至若鹿野龙宫之秘，猿江鹤树之文，莫不递贯清衷，总持丹府。班荆坐樾，独神王于亭皋；朗啸长吟，乃情超于宇宙。蒲轮每至，攀桂之节逾高；玉帛屡陈，枕石之诚弥固。遂乃缅怀飞遁，抗迹崂山；托岫疏阶，凭林结栋。纫兰制芰，方轻藻火之衣；爽籁风松，自代管弦之响。横经者四集，请益者千馀，高凤愧以韬光，张超谢其成市。于时南风不竞，东土构屯，人厌豺狼之毒，家充虵豕之铒。盗仍有道，望境归仁，共结盟誓之言。不犯征君之界，岂非至诚攸感，木石开心者乎。及元历告终，青光启祚，齐高祖希风伫德，侧席傍求，屡下征书，确乎不拔。其后又移居郁洲掩榆山栖云精舍，情亲鱼鸟，志狎烟霞，蜕影樊笼，萧然独往。齐建元元年，又下诏征为散骑侍郎，又不就。既而济岱沦胥，公私荡覆，稽天之浸，将湮蹈海之居；燎原之火，欲烬藏山之璞。乃鸿骞凤举，腾万仞以高翔，择木选君，相九土而遥集。凌江迥憩，遂届南京。负杖泉邱，游睨林壑，历观胜境，行次摄山，神谷仙岩，特符心赏。夜间披榛草，定迹深栖，树槿疏池，有终焉之志。此山其状如伞，故亦号曰伞山。丹穴红泉，共星河而竞泻，珠林镜𪩘，与月桂而交辉。鸟哢严虚，猿吟涧静。松门杳霭。去来千里之云，花楦丰茸，含吐十枝之日。实息心之胜地，乃宴坐之名区。爰集法流，于焉讲肄。音容秀澈，宇量端凝，投论会奇，兴言入妙。若洪锺之虚受，有击必扬；似明镜之忘疲，无来不应。于时元儒兼阐，道俗同归，俱号净名，以旌至德。先是山多猛噬，人罕登临。昇岩有仙谷之危，越涧等凭河之险。征君心不忤物，总万类以敷仁，故使物乃革心，屏三毒而归惠。兴风敛暴，遽承探鲠之恩；游雾含辛，自埒报珠之感。于时齐道方修，寤寐求贤。永明元年，又征为国子博士。征君隐居求志，义越乎由光；不降凝心，迹高于园绮。凿坏贞遁，漱石忘归，鹤版载临，豹姿逾远。俄有法师僧辩，承风景慕，翼徒振锡，翻然戾止。法师业隆三藏，道迈四依，戒行坚明，律轨严净。欣然一遇，叶契干龄。子琴为莫逆之交，温雪岂容声之友。因即邻岩构宇，别起梵居。耸峤飞柯，含风吐雾，栖霞之寺，由此创名。

福地裁基，肇发初心之誓；法门构邃，遽锺后说之辰。安居顷之，辩师迁化。六年顶拜，虽开青石之坛；千日威光，未建紫金之岳。征君积缘登妙，至感入微。尝梦法身，冠于曾𪩘，后因垂眺，屣步林亭。乃有浮磬吟空，写圆音于帷树；飞香散迥，腾宝气于炉峰。又睹真颜于岩之首，神光骇瞩，若登灵鹫之山；妙力难思，如游瞽龙之邑。岂止无垢佛国，独荫珠云；净德王家，方承珂雪。是知不行而至，冥通应感之符；为法而来，实昭光启之福。非夫慧因宿植，其孰与于此哉！于是拜受嘉征，愿言经始，将于岩壁，造大尊仪。乃眷为山，未遑初篑。遽而西州智士，与晓岳而俱倾；东国高人，随夜星而共没。琼瑶落彩，峰岫沈晖。永明二年，奄迁舟壑。第二子临沂公仲璋，顾慕曾峦，既崩心于岵望；徘徊曩构，更泣血于楹书。遂琢彼翠屏，爰开叶座，舍兹碧题，式建花宫。上宪优填之区，仰镂能仁之像；校美何充之宅，遽兴崇德之闱。逖彼萧宗，大宏释典，文惠太子及竟陵王，或澄少海之源派，朝宗于法海；或茂本枝之颖发，萌柢于禅枝。咸舍净财，光隆慧业。时有沙门法度，为智殿之栋梁，即此旧基，更兴新制。又造尊像十有馀龛，及梁运载兴，锐心回向，大林精舍，并事庄严。临川王载剖竹符，宣化惟扬之境；言寻奈苑，兴想拔茅之义。以天监一十五载造无量寿像一区，带地连光，合高五丈。满月之瑞，湛珠镜以出云崖；聚日之辉，昇璧轮而皎烟路。参差四注，周以鸟翅之房；迢遰千寻，饰以鱼鳞之瓦。击鸣干于爽籁，则步影齐归；丽停午于高曦，则息心攸萃。逾锦城而特建，掩银界而孤标。良由积慧所符，大士著甚深之业，用能遥诚克果，永代增希有之缘。以旷劫之隆因，开舍生之至福。伟哉壮观，无得而称。

朕肃纂祯图，丕承宝历，澄九溟而有截，宴八表而无为。紫塞丹岑，接封畿于上苑；白门青野，款賝贽于仙闱。将使率土苍生，镇昇仁寿之域，普天黔首，永蹈淳古之源。崇庆越于两仪，景运逾于万劫。属以冕旒多暇，物色傍求，瞻江海而载怀，咏林泉而兴想。钦风味道，恨不同时。古往今来，抚运化而虽寂，德崇业著，眷神理而犹存。寤寐遗尘，有兼前烈，瞻言胜轨，叹伫唯深。今故于彼度人，常满七七，各兼衣钵钱二百贯，绢二百匹，苏参拾斛，绣像织成像新旧翻译一切经一藏，并幡华等物。凭幽寻之曩迹，光显德门；托嘉遁之名区，追崇仁里。就福宇而延福，即祥基以缔祥。冀缘金囿之庭，近叶珠囊之耀，所愿通因法岸，契果禅林，九鼎与元极同安，七庙与紫微齐固。总三千之净土，并沐薰歌；罄百亿之恒沙，长为寿算；铁围之所苞括，玉烛之所照临，常餐六气之和，俱藻一音之听。夫象以尽意，意非象而不申；言以会情，情非言而不畅。是以发挥二谛，宏演四依，迥讬莲花之峰，遥刻芝英之字，庶海桑频变，孤超弇岳之碑，城芥屡空，独跨稽岑之篆。式陈茂实，乃作铭云：

悠悠法界，总总含生。轮回欲海，起灭身城。俱安大夜，共习无明。爱尘岳聚，毒树云平。（其一）

邈矣遍知，超然独悟。遽乘五演，高被六度。大空善说，中天巧谕。引彼迷途，归之觉路。（其二）

猗欤净行，育彩昆田。家承珪组，代著忠贤。戒支宿习，种智斯圆。栋梁三宝，薰修四禅。（其三）

爰始筮宾，薜萝攸整。蹈海沈迹，栖岩灭影。天地构屯，干戈互警。北林罔庇，南辕载骋。（其四）

翻飞泽国，历考山图。言瞻碧磴，自韫元珠。凭峰架室，枕壑通衢。鳣庭广跨，马帐宏敷。（其五）

同气相求，善邻遥讬。道符久敬，心均常乐。对辟金园，并疏银阁。谷停帝马，峦归梵鹤。（其六）

空分瑞塔，地积香台。珂月霄英，珠云旦来。千光雾起，七净霞闻。谷边飞锡，涧下乘杯。（其七）

桂𪩘参差，松亭隐霭。石坛照锦，瑶泉泻籁。岫接香炉，峰承宝盖。翔凫演法，毒龙销害。（其八）

梵宫既启，福海长深。噬虺忘穴，飞鸮革音。群生普戴，奕祀同钦。不有高节，宁符宿心。（其九）

御扆多闲，闻风逖想。茂轨遐劭，清晖遽往。伫契业于圆明，冀崇缘于方广。镂飞篆于曾岳，齐胜基于穹壤。（其十）

English Translation

I have heard that the jade towers of Mount Zhong were places through which the feathered carriages of immortals roamed, and that the golden terraces of Mount Kun were places where the feather-robed transcendents gathered. Yet although the true doctrine of the immortals is remote and obscure, and its divine principles subtle and faint, they still remain within the Three Realms and have not passed beyond the Nine Heavens.

Only the Vehicle of Thusness extends without limit, while wondrous enlightenment is perfect and radiant. Relying upon the principle of non-arising, it delivers those who possess life; taking the formless as its basis, it brings forth the Great Image. Its Way transcends the boundary between coming and going, so no verbal device can grasp its origin. Its principle leaves behind the workings of movement and stillness, so neither pursuit nor reception can disclose its depths. Those who find its gate are as rare as those who encounter the jewel concealed in a royal topknot; those who reach its ultimate end are as uncommon as the blossoming of the uḍumbara flower.

During the Southern Qi dynasty there lived the summoned recluse Ming Sengshao, a native of Pingyuan. Zhongyong engendered his long line of descendants; Jingbo spread its numinous shoots; and the fragrant source of the family began with Mengming, from whom the clan took Ming as its surname.

His great-grandfather Chen served under the Jin dynasty as a Gentleman in the Bureau of Historiography. His grandfather Wan served under the Jin as General Who Establishes Might. The phoenix-like refinement of their learning spread their reputation, and their elegant character stood eminent among the Confucian scholars; their leopard-like military strategy displayed its power, and their fragrant fame rose above the officers of the military academies.

His father Lüe served under the Liu Song dynasty as Administrator of Pingyuan and Palace Secretariat Attendant. When sent out to govern, he spread benevolent instruction that benefited all around him; when entrusted with the imperial decrees, he exhausted his loyal counsel in service to the sovereign.

From an early age, the Summoned Gentleman planted pure causes and carried within himself the seeds of wisdom accumulated in former lives. In the years of patterned youth, he awakened to true emptiness; while still wearing the blue collar of a student, he understood the nature of the Dharma. His insight communicated with the numinous, and his mind accorded with the Way.

He cast aside the honors of carriages and official caps, having never in youth possessed feelings contaminated by worldly dust. He entrusted his aspirations to forests and cliffs, naturally conforming to the secluded integrity of the recluse. His lofty and exceptional vitality eclipsed the jade-like moon and rose into the crimson heavens; his brilliant purity washed itself in jeweled torrents and soared above emerald rapids.

Within appearances he perceived non-appearance and pointed to the ten thousand phenomena as emptiness. Recognizing neither self nor person, he regarded the four currents of worldly existence as equal in quiescent extinction.

Moreover, he exhausted the full range of Confucian learning and comprehensively mastered the ancient canons. His discussions reached the ultimate ford of profound mystery, and he thoroughly understood the Laozi and the Book of Changes. As for the secrets of the Deer Park and the Dragon Palace, and the writings associated with the Ape River and the Crane Trees, there was nothing that did not pass successively through his lucid mind and become completely retained within his inner treasury.

Spreading brushwood as a mat and sitting beneath the shade of trees, his spirit alone reigned supreme over the open countryside. When he gave a clear whistle or prolonged chant, his feelings passed beyond the universe. Imperial carriages with wheels wrapped in rushes repeatedly came to summon him, yet his integrity as one who grasped the cassia branch of reclusion only rose higher. Gifts of jade and silk were repeatedly presented, yet his sincerity in resting his head upon the rocks became still firmer.

He therefore turned his thoughts toward distant withdrawal and raised his traces above the world on Mount Lao. Relying upon a mountain recess, he cut steps into the slope; supported by the forest, he joined timbers to construct a dwelling. He threaded orchids and fashioned garments from water plants, regarding the ceremonial robes ornamented with algae and flames as things of little worth. The clear sounds of wind passing through the pines replaced for him the music of flutes and strings.

Those who came carrying the classics gathered from all directions, and more than a thousand came to request instruction. Even Gao Feng would have been ashamed to conceal his brilliance before him, while Zhang Chao would have yielded to the crowds that made the place resemble a marketplace.

At that time, the power of the south was failing and the eastern lands were thrown into disorder. The people were sickened by the cruelty of jackals and wolves, and households became prey for serpents and wild boars. Yet even the brigands retained some sense of the Way. Looking toward the borders of his domain, they submitted to his benevolence and together swore an oath never to violate the territory of the Summoned Gentleman. Was this not because his perfect sincerity produced a response so profound that even wood and stone would have opened their hearts?

When the former calendar came to an end and the azure radiance of the Qi dynasty inaugurated its rule, Emperor Gaozu of Qi admired his character and waited upon his virtue. Leaving the seat beside him vacant, the emperor sought him in every direction and repeatedly issued summonses, but he remained firmly unmovable.

He later moved to the Qiyun Monastery on Yanyu Mountain in Yuzhou. He treated fish and birds as intimate companions and took mist and clouds as his familiar friends. Having shed every trace of the confining cage, he went his solitary way in complete freedom.

In the first year of the Jianyuan era, an edict once more summoned him to serve as a Cavalier Attendant, but again he did not accept the appointment.

Before long, the territories around the Ji River and Mount Tai were together engulfed, and both public and private property were swept away. Floodwaters reaching the heavens were about to submerge his dwelling beside the sea, while flames spreading across the plains threatened to consume the jade concealed within the mountain. He therefore rose like a great wild goose and lifted himself like a phoenix, soaring ten thousand fathoms into the sky. Like a bird selecting its tree and a worthy man choosing his ruler, he surveyed the Nine Regions from afar.

Crossing the great river, he rested at a distant place and eventually arrived at the southern capital. Carrying his staff among springs and hills, he wandered through forests and valleys and inspected the finest landscapes. During his travels he came to Mount She. Its divine valleys and immortal cliffs corresponded especially well with what delighted his heart.

He cleared away brambles and grass, established his traces in deep seclusion, planted hibiscus trees, and opened a pond, intending to remain there until the end of his life. Because the mountain was shaped like an umbrella, it was also called Umbrella Mountain.

Crimson caverns and red springs poured forth in competition with the River of Stars. Pearl-like forests and mirrorlike crags shone together with the cassia tree of the moon. Birds sang among the empty cliffs, and gibbons called through the silent ravines. The gateway of pines disappeared into the distant haze, receiving clouds that had traveled a thousand li. Luxuriant flowering trees seemed to swallow and release the ten-branched sun.

It was truly an excellent place for calming the mind and a celebrated region for seated meditation. There he gathered those who followed the stream of the Dharma and instructed them in Buddhist learning.

His voice and appearance were refined and lucid, while his bearing was upright and composed. When arguments were presented to him, he grasped their extraordinary points; whenever he spoke, his words entered the wondrous. He was like a great bell whose emptiness enables it to receive every blow and resound whenever struck. He was like a bright mirror that never tires, responding to everything that comes before it.

At that time, he expounded both the teachings of profound mystery and the learning of the Confucians. Clerics and laypeople alike returned to him, and all called him Vimalakīrti in recognition of his supreme virtue.

Previously, the mountain had contained many savage beasts, and few people dared to climb it. Ascending its cliffs involved the dangers of an immortal valley, while crossing its ravines was as perilous as relying upon the River of Heaven.

The Summoned Gentleman’s heart was never hostile toward any creature. He embraced the ten thousand species and extended benevolence to all. Consequently, even the beasts transformed their hearts, put aside the Three Poisons, and submitted to his kindness. Those that raised the wind restrained their violence, as though remembering the favor of having an obstruction removed from their throats; those that wandered through the mist endured hardship, as though repaying a benefactor with a pearl.

At that time, the Way of Qi was flourishing, and the court sought worthy men both waking and sleeping. In the first year of the Yongming era, he was again summoned to serve as Erudite of the Directorate of Education.

The Summoned Gentleman, however, remained in seclusion in order to preserve his purpose. In righteousness he surpassed Xu You and Wu Guang; in his refusal to submit and in his concentration of mind, his conduct rose above that of the recluses Dongyuan Gong and Qili Ji. Maintaining the pure withdrawal of one who would break through a wall to escape worldly service, and rinsing stones until he forgot to return, he received another summons inscribed upon the crane tablet, yet his leopard-like figure withdrew still farther away.

Not long afterward, the Dharma master Sengbian heard of his reputation and admired his example. Leading his disciples and raising his monk’s staff, he traveled swiftly and arrived there.

The Dharma master’s mastery of the Tripiṭaka was profound, his attainment in the Way surpassed the Four Reliances, his observance of the precepts was firm and radiant, and his disciplinary practice was strict and pure. The two rejoiced upon meeting, and their agreement seemed to have been formed across a thousand years. They became friends of perfect mutual understanding, a friendship that even the celebrated companions of antiquity could not have surpassed.

Sengbian therefore constructed buildings beside the neighboring cliffs and separately founded a Buddhist monastery. Its buildings rose over the peaks, while its projecting eaves seemed to fly; it contained the wind and breathed forth mist. From this foundation the Temple of Qixia received its name.

The foundation of this blessed ground had only just been laid, giving initial expression to a long-held vow, and the profound gateway of the Dharma had only just been constructed when the hour of the master’s final teaching suddenly arrived. After residing there only a short time, Master Bian passed into transformation.

Although the blue-stone altar had been opened for years of prostration, and a thousand days of awe-inspiring radiance had passed, the purple-gold mountain of a great Buddha image had not yet been erected.

The Summoned Gentleman had accumulated conditions that led toward the wondrous, and his deepest sincerity penetrated the most subtle realm. He once dreamed that the Dharma body of the Buddha towered above a lofty peak.

Later, while looking into the distance, he walked in light shoes through a forest pavilion. Suddenly a floating stone chime sounded in the empty air, spreading the Buddha’s perfect voice among the curtain-like trees. Fragrance flew outward and dispersed into the distance, while a precious vapor rose over the incense-burner-shaped peak.

He then saw the true countenance of the Buddha above the summit of the cliff. Divine light dazzled his eyes. It was as though he had ascended Vulture Peak; its wondrous power was inconceivable, as though he had traveled through the city of the blind dragons.

How could this have been only the jeweled cloud that shaded the stainless Buddha-land, or merely the white-jade snow received by the household of King Pure Virtue? From this we know that to arrive without traveling was a sign of the hidden communication between stimulus and response; to come for the sake of the Dharma truly revealed the blessing that would inaugurate its radiance. Had he not planted the causes of wisdom in former lives, how could he have participated in such a manifestation?

He therefore bowed and accepted the auspicious sign. He declared his vow to begin the undertaking and intended to carve a great sacred image into the cliff face.

Yet although he turned his concern toward the mountain, he had not had time even to raise the first basket of earth when the wise man of the western region fell together with the mountain of dawn, and the lofty man of the eastern lands disappeared together with the stars of night. Jewels lost their brilliance, and the peaks and ridges sank into darkness.

In the second year of the Yongming era, he suddenly passed away, like a boat secretly removed from a ravine.

His second son, Zhongzhang, Duke of Linyi, looked toward the lofty ridges with grief and longing. His heart broke as he gazed toward the paternal hill, and as he wandered among the structures formerly planned by his father, he wept tears of blood before the writings upon the pillars.

He therefore carved into the emerald screen of rock and opened a lotus throne. Giving up his green-gabled residence, he established a palace of flowers. Above, he took as his model the sanctuary in which King Udayana had fashioned the first image of the Buddha, and reverently carved an image of the Able and Benevolent One, Śākyamuni. Rivaling the beauty of the residence donated by He Chong, he immediately raised a sanctuary devoted to exalted virtue.

The distant descendants of the imperial house of Xiao greatly promoted the Buddhist scriptures. Crown Prince Wenhui and the Prince of Jingling either clarified the streams flowing from the Lesser Sea and directed them toward the ocean of the Dharma, or caused the flourishing branches of the imperial house to send their roots into the tree of meditation. All donated pure wealth and gloriously enlarged the works of wisdom.

At that time, the monk Fadu served as a pillar and roof beam of the palace of wisdom. Upon the existing foundation he created a new design and constructed more than ten niches containing sacred images.

When the fortune of the Liang dynasty arose, its rulers devoted themselves earnestly to the turning of the mind toward enlightenment, and the great woodland monastery was adorned in every respect.

The Prince of Linchuan then received the bamboo tally of regional authority and spread transforming instruction through the lands of Yangzhou. Seeking the mango grove of the Buddha, he conceived the shared purpose symbolized by the pulling of joined grass roots.

In the fifteenth year of the Tianjian era, he constructed an image of Amitāyus. Connected with the earth and joined with radiance, it reached a total height of five zhang.

The auspicious countenance of the full moon emerged from the cloudy cliff like a clear pearl mirror. The gathered radiance of many suns rose like a jade disk and illuminated the road through the mist.

Unevenly arranged structures extended in four directions, surrounding the image with chambers like the wings of birds. Rising into the distance for what seemed a thousand fathoms, they were ornamented with roof tiles like the scales of fish.

When the wooden striker sounded through the clear air, the shadows of approaching footsteps returned together. When the brilliant sun stood at the height of noon, those who sought to calm their minds gathered there.

It surpassed the distinguished monuments of Brocade City and stood alone, outshining the Silver Realm. This was truly because accumulated wisdom had produced a corresponding sign, and because the Great Being had established a most profound work. Through this, distant sincerity succeeded in bearing fruit, and rare conditions were increased for generations to come.

By means of exalted causes accumulated through immeasurable kalpas, it opened the supreme merit of relinquishing one’s own life. Magnificent indeed was this great spectacle: no words could adequately describe it.

I have reverently succeeded to the auspicious imperial inheritance and received the precious calendar of rule. I have brought clarity to the Nine Seas and united their people, and I govern the Eight Regions peacefully through non-action.

Purple passes and crimson peaks connect the imperial territory with the upper gardens. The White Gate and green fields welcome those who present treasures and tribute before the immortal palace.

I intend that all living people throughout the realm shall continually ascend into the region of benevolence and long life, and that all the black-haired people beneath Heaven shall forever walk within the source of ancient simplicity. May this exalted blessing surpass Heaven and Earth, and may this splendid fortune extend beyond ten thousand kalpas.

As the duties associated with the imperial crown have allowed me some leisure, I have searched in every direction for worthy men. Looking toward the rivers and seas, I have cherished thoughts of them; chanting of forests and springs, I have conceived a longing for the recluse.

I revere his character and savor his Way, regretting that I did not live in his time. Although antiquity has passed and the present has come, and although, following the transformations of destiny, he has entered stillness, his virtue was exalted, his work was manifest, and in contemplating the divine principle one finds that he still survives.

Waking and sleeping, I have thought of the traces he left behind, which stand together with the achievements of the worthy men of former generations. Looking toward his victorious example, I have remained in profound admiration.

I therefore command that forty-nine persons regularly be ordained at that place. I additionally grant robes and bowls, two hundred strings of cash, two hundred bolts of silk, thirty hu of clarified butter, embroidered images, woven images, one complete collection of all scriptures in both the old and new translations, together with banners, flowers, and other objects.

Relying upon the former traces found in this secluded place, I seek to make his virtuous lineage shine. Entrusting these gifts to the celebrated region of his noble withdrawal, I honor the village of his benevolence.

At this sanctuary of merit, I extend merit; upon this auspicious foundation, I establish further auspiciousness. I hope that, through the garden of gold, we may approach the radiance of the jeweled repository of scripture. I pray that we may pass through the causes leading to the shore of the Dharma and accord with their fruits in the forest of meditation.

May the Nine Tripods of the state remain secure together with the Supreme Pole, and may the Seven Ancestral Temples remain firm together with the Purple Tenuity Palace of Heaven.

May all the pure lands of the three-thousandfold great-thousand world together receive the blessings of fragrant hymns. May all the sands of the Ganges throughout the hundred billion worlds forever be added to the measure of the imperial lifespan.

All that is enclosed by the Iron Encircling Mountains and illuminated by the harmonious Jade Candle shall continually partake of the harmony of the Six Vital Forces and together receive the teaching of the Buddha’s single voice.

Images are used to exhaust meaning, yet without images meaning cannot be displayed. Words are used to communicate feeling, yet without words feeling cannot be expressed.

Therefore, in order to illuminate the Two Truths and broadly expound the Four Reliances, I entrust this text to the distant Lotus-Blossom Peak and carve these numinous words upon it.

May it stand alone above the stele of Mount Yan even after seas have repeatedly become mulberry fields; may its seal-script inscription surpass that of the peaks of Kuaiji even after cities filled with mustard seeds have repeatedly been emptied.

Thus I set forth these abundant realities and compose the inscription:

Boundless is the realm of the Dharma;
Crowded are all beings within it.
They revolve through the ocean of desire,
As bodies arise and perish in the city of existence.
Together they rest in the great night;
Together they practice ignorance.
The dust of attachment gathers like mountains;
The trees of poison rise level with the clouds.
I

Remote and exalted is the All-Knowing One;
Transcendent, he awakened alone.
He immediately mounted the Five Vehicles
And broadly spread the Six Perfections.
He skillfully taught the Great Emptiness
And gave wondrous instruction beneath the central heavens.
He guided those who had entered the road of delusion
And returned them to the path of enlightenment.
II

Admirable was this practitioner of purity,
Whose brilliance was nurtured in the jade fields of Kunlun.
His family inherited jade insignia and official ribbons;
For generations it was celebrated for loyalty and worth.
The supports of moral discipline had been cultivated in former lives,
And in him the seed of wisdom became complete.
He served as a roof beam and pillar of the Three Jewels
And cultivated the fragrant discipline of the Four Meditations.
III

Then he first divined a place of residence
And arranged his garments of vines and creepers.
He concealed his traces beside the sea
And extinguished his shadow among the cliffs.
Heaven and Earth fell into disorder;
Weapons of war sounded warnings on every side.
The forests of the north could no longer shelter him,
And his carriage turned southward and began its journey.
IV

He flew across the lands of rivers and marshes
And carefully examined the maps of the mountains.
Looking toward the emerald steps,
He concealed within himself the mysterious pearl.
Against the peak he raised his dwelling;
Beside the valley he opened a road.
The courtyard of learning extended broadly,
And the teaching tent spread without limit.
V

Those of the same vital spirit sought one another;
A virtuous neighbor entrusted himself from afar.
Their Ways accorded in enduring reverence,
And their minds shared constant joy.
Facing one another, they opened the Golden Garden
And together cleared the Silver Pavilion.
The valley detained the imperial horses;
The ridges received the sacred cranes of Buddhism.
VI

An auspicious stūpa appeared against the emptiness;
The earth accumulated terraces of fragrance.
A jade-like moon shone through the night sky,
And clouds of pearls arrived with the dawn.
A thousand rays rose through the mist;
The radiance of the Seven Purities was heard.
Beside the valley, monks traveled with flying staffs;
Below the ravine, holy men crossed upon floating cups.
VII

Cassia-covered peaks rose at uneven heights;
Pine pavilions disappeared into hidden mist.
Stone altars shone like patterned brocade;
Jade springs poured forth musical sounds.
The ridges joined the Incense-Burner Peak;
The summits supported a jeweled canopy.
Flying waterfowl proclaimed the Dharma;
Poisonous dragons cast away their harmful nature.
VIII

Once the Buddhist palace had been opened,
Its ocean of merit grew forever deeper.
Venomous serpents forgot their caves;
Flying owls transformed their cries.
All living beings universally received its protection,
And successive generations honored it together.
Had there not been such lofty integrity,
How could the vow of former lives have been fulfilled?
IX

When leisure came before the imperial screen,
I heard of his character and thought of him from afar.
His magnificent example reached distant ages,
Yet his pure radiance had suddenly departed.
I wait in hope of joining his work of perfect illumination
And seek to increase the conditions leading to the Vaipulya teachings.
I carve these flying seal-script characters upon the lofty mountain,
Making this victorious foundation endure with Heaven and Earth.

Interpretation

Imperial history written into stone.

The inscription should be interpreted not as a neutral biography, but as a Tang imperial reconstruction of Qixia Temple’s past. It presents Ming Sengshao’s refusal of office as moral purity, then absorbs that independent reputation into imperial ideology: the ideal ruler proves his virtue by recognizing and honoring worthy recluses. It also shows that Qixia Temple was created through cooperation among aristocratic families, monks, and royal patrons rather than by one founder alone. Finally, the inscription connects Buddhist merit with political order, dynastic stability, and imperial longevity. Its central message is that supporting Buddhism could strengthen both the moral authority of the emperor and the legitimacy of the state.

Historical Background

From a recluse’s retreat to a dynastic monument.

This inscription is the Ming Zhengjun Stele of Qixia Temple, erected under Tang emperor Gaozong in 676 CE. However, it describes events beginning nearly two centuries earlier, during the Southern Qi and Liang dynasties.

Ming Sengshao was an aristocratic scholar and Buddhist recluse who repeatedly refused government appointments. After warfare forced him to leave northern China, he settled at Mount She near Jiankang, modern Nanjing. There he established a retreat and attracted monks, scholars, and students. The monk Sengbian later founded a monastery beside his residence, creating the early Qixia Temple.

Ming planned to carve a large Buddhist image into the mountain but died in 484 before completing it. His son Ming Zhongzhang, the monk Fadu, and members of the Southern Qi and Liang royal families continued the project, producing the earliest Qixia grottoes.

The Tang court later erected this stele to honor Ming Sengshao, support Qixia Temple, and connect Buddhist patronage with imperial legitimacy, social order, and the protection of the dynasty.

August 1, 951 · Southern Tang

Record of the New Road to Qixia Temple at Mount She

《摄山栖霞寺新路记》 · 徐铉

Texts Interpretation Background Figures

Chinese Text

栖霞寺山水胜绝，景象瑰奇，明征君故宅在焉，江令公旧碑详矣。高宗大帝刊圣藻于贞石，纡宸翰于璿题，焕乎天光，被此幽谷。

先是兹山之距都也，五十里而遥，方轨并驱，崇朝可至。及中原构乱，多垒在郊，野无牧马之童，歧有亡羊之仆。义祖武皇帝潜龙兹邑，访道来游，始命有司，是作新路。金椎既隐，玉辂言还。桐山之驾不追，回中之道亦废。呜呼！圣人遗迹，必将不泯。微禹之叹，夫何逮哉！

保大辛亥岁，时安岁丰，政简民暇。粤有寺僧道严，名高白足，动思利人。百姓庄思悰，家擅素封，积而能散。嗟亭堠之不复，悯行旅之多艰。乃相与翦荆榛，疏坎窞，辟通衢之夷直，弃邪径之迂回，建高亭于道周，跨重桥于川上。凿甘井以救暍，立石表以指迷。草树风烟，依依四望；峰峦台榭，肃肃前瞻。由是江乘之涂，复识王畿之制矣。

余职事多暇，屡游此山。喜直道之攸遵，嘉二叟之不懈。为文刻石，用纪成功。俾后之好事者，以时开通，随坏完葺。此碣有泐，斯文未湮，不亦美乎？

其年八月一日，兵部员外郎知制诰徐铉记。

English Translation

Qixia Temple possesses mountains and waters of unsurpassed beauty, and its landscape is extraordinary. The former residence of the recluse Ming Sengshao stands here, as the old stele written by Lord Jiang has already explained in detail. Emperor Gaozong also engraved his imperial composition upon enduring stone. His calligraphy shines like heavenly light throughout this secluded valley.

Mount She lies approximately fifty li from the capital. Its road was once broad enough for two carriages to travel side by side, and the journey could be completed within a morning. Later, however, warfare brought disorder to the Central Plain. Fortifications filled the outskirts, no herdboys remained in the fields, and travelers became lost among the branching paths.

When Emperor Yizu Wu was still living in this region before his rise, he visited the mountain in search of the Way and ordered the responsible officials to construct a new road. After the imperial patron had departed, however, the road gradually fell into disuse. The imperial carriage no longer returned, and the former route was abandoned. Yet the traces left by an honored ruler should not be allowed to disappear.

In the xinhai year of the Baoda era, the country was peaceful, the harvest was abundant, government affairs were light, and the people had time to undertake public work.

A monk of Qixia Temple named Daoyan was highly respected among the Buddhist clergy and constantly considered how he might benefit others. A local resident named Zhuang Sicong came from a wealthy family but was willing to distribute what he had accumulated.

They lamented the disappearance of the roadside stations and sympathized with the difficulties faced by travelers. Together, they cut away thorns and undergrowth, cleared pits and hollows, opened a broad and level road, and abandoned the indirect side paths.

They built a tall pavilion beside the road and constructed a substantial bridge across the stream. They dug a well of fresh water to relieve travelers suffering from heat and erected stone markers to guide those who had lost their way.

Trees, mist, and wind could now be viewed in every direction, while the mountain peaks and temple buildings appeared solemnly ahead. Through this work, the road through Jiangcheng once again displayed the order appropriate to the territory surrounding a capital.

My official responsibilities sometimes leave me with leisure, and I have visited this mountain repeatedly. I am pleased that travelers can now follow a direct road, and I admire the persistence of these two elders.

I have therefore composed this text and ordered it to be carved in stone to commemorate the completion of their work. May later public-spirited people reopen the route when necessary and repair it whenever it is damaged.

Even if this stone tablet eventually becomes worn, may the meaning of this text never disappear. Would that not be a worthy achievement?

Recorded by Xu Xuan, Vice Director in the Ministry of War and Drafter of Imperial Proclamations, on the first day of the eighth month of that year.

Interpretation

A road as moral and political order.

The first road was ordered by a ruler, but its restoration depended upon a monk and a wealthy local donor. Xu Xuan then preserved their achievement in an official inscription. Qixia’s accessibility therefore joined political authority, monastic initiative, and private patronage.

The bridge, well, pavilion, and direction markers served ordinary travelers as well as worshippers, turning Buddhist compassion into practical public service. By claiming that the road restored the “order of the royal domain,” the inscription also made safe movement around the capital a sign of effective government. Its final appeal for continuing repairs presented infrastructure maintenance as an ongoing public responsibility.

Historical Background

Qixia within the Southern Tang capital region.

Xu Xuan wrote this inscription in 951, when Jinling—modern Nanjing—was the Southern Tang capital. Qixia Temple stood about fifty li outside the city and relied on a usable road for monks, worshippers, officials, and other travelers.

An earlier road had been commissioned by Xu Wen, later honored by the Southern Tang court as Emperor Yizu Wu. After the route deteriorated, the monk Daoyan and the local donor Zhuang Sicong organized a straighter road, bridge, roadside pavilion, well, and stone direction markers. Xu Xuan commemorated the project and urged later generations to maintain it.
