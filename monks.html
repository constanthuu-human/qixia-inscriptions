People · Networks · Actions

Monks in the Jiankang story

These figures are presented through what they did: translating, teaching, founding, organizing, travelling, protecting, and carrying religious authority between people and places.

A connected cast

Different monks solved different problems.

No single monk “created” Nanjing Buddhism. The city’s religious history formed through overlapping actions and institutions.

Dates and biographies are unevenly documented. Where a story comes from later tradition, it is labelled as tradition.

Eastern Wu · Translator and organizer

Kang Senghui

康僧会

Associated with the early Buddhist establishment at Jianye and the Jianchu Temple tradition. Stories about relics and court recognition made his work legible to a new political community.

Evidence: early biographies and later temple traditions; the exact arrival date remains debated.

Eastern Wu · Translator

Zhi Qian

支谦

Translated Buddhist texts into literary Chinese for readers in the Eastern Wu world. His work made foreign teachings available within established Chinese intellectual language.

Network: translation, court culture, texts, and educated lay audiences.

Southern Qi · Founder tradition

Ming Sengshao

明僧绍

Retreated to Mount She and is remembered for giving his residence for the foundation of Qixia Temple. A private elite retreat became a long-lived monastic landscape.

Evidence: Qixia inscriptions and later temple histories.

Southern Dynasties · Teacher

Fadu

法度

Associated with the organization of Qixia’s early community and the transmission of Buddhist learning. His role represents the work between a donor’s gift and a functioning monastery.

Evidence: local foundation narratives; details should be checked against the inscription archive.

Liang · Ritual specialist

Baozhi

宝志

Known through biographies and miracle traditions connected with the Liang court. His reputation linked a charismatic monk, imperial patronage, and the sacred landscape later associated with Linggu.

Read miracle stories as religious memory, not as simple administrative records.

Sui–Tang · Teacher

Zhiyi

智顗

His Tiantai teaching shaped later ways of organizing Buddhist doctrine, practice, and scripture. The Nanjing connection belongs to a wider network of travel and transmission.

Network: teachers, scriptures, pilgrimage, and regional monasteries.

Sui–Tang · Recorder

Guanding

灌顶

Recorded and organized Zhiyi’s teachings. His work shows that a Buddhist school depended on editors, disciples, and institutional memory as much as on a single teacher.

Action: preserving teachings so they could circulate beyond one monastery.

Modern · Protector

Jiran

寂然

Remembered in Qixia’s modern history for protecting civilians who sought refuge at the temple during wartime. His story expands the meaning of a monastery’s public role.

Evidence: modern temple memory and wartime accounts; compare sources carefully.

How to follow a person

Start with an action, then follow its connections.

Choose one figure and ask four questions: Where did the person live or travel? What problem did the person face? Who supported the action? What changed afterward?

For example, follow Ming Sengshao from elite retreat to Qixia’s founding tradition; then move from the founder to monks, sculptors, donors, and writers who made the site continue.
