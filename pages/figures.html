Kang Senghui · 康僧会

Traditional biographies connect Kang Senghui with the arrival of Buddhism at Jianye, the Jianchu Temple tradition and the use of relic devotion to gain public recognition. His story places a travelling monk, a new religious institution and political authority in the same historical frame.

Evidence level: early tradition with disputed chronology. The often-cited arrival date of 247 should be labelled as traditional rather than certain.

Why he matters

Kang Senghui lets the project ask how Buddhism became institutional rather than remaining a collection of individual beliefs. His profile links the Eastern Wu map to the translation archive and to the question of how religious authority was recognized at court.

Research starting point: Ito, “Three Versions of Kang Senghui’s Biographies.”

Ming Sengshao · 明僧绍

Ming Sengshao is remembered as a scholar-recluse associated with Mount She. His residence became connected with Qixia’s foundation, turning a private retreat into a long-lived religious landscape of temples, caves, sculpture and inscriptions.

Evidence level: documented through later historical inscriptions and temple traditions; individual details should be cited separately.

Why he matters

His profile shifts the story away from emperors alone. It shows how elite donation, mountain retreat culture and monastic organization could work together to create a Buddhist institution.

Research starting point: Nanjing University, “These Jiangsu Sites Take You Back to the Tang.”

Baozhi · 宝志

Baozhi was a celebrated Liang monk whose reputation became tied to Emperor Wu’s patronage. The Kaishan Temple tradition connected his burial and memory to a site that later developed into the Linggu religious landscape.

Evidence level: strong connection in local heritage narratives; distinguish the monk’s historical biography from later miracle traditions.

Why he matters

Baozhi shows how the memory of one religious specialist could become an institutional landscape. The temple continued through renaming, relocation and reconstruction even as the original political circumstances changed.

Research starting point: Nanjing Zhongshan Scenic Area, Linggu history.

Zhu Yuanzhang · 朱元璋

Zhu Yuanzhang’s involvement came through imperial planning. When the site selected for the Ming Xiaoling complex overlapped with the earlier monastery landscape, the institution was relocated and renamed Linggu Temple.

Evidence level: documented institutional relocation; do not describe the present temple as an unchanged survival of the earlier site.

Why he matters

He demonstrates how imperial power could reshape Buddhist geography without simply eliminating the institution. Relocation created both continuity and rupture: the religious identity remained, but the physical setting changed.

Research starting point: Nanjing Zhongshan Scenic Area, Linggu history.

Zheng He · 郑和

Zheng He’s connection belongs to the maritime and commemorative history of Jinghai Temple. His voyages became part of an imperial religious landscape involving ceremony, protection at sea and public memory.

Evidence level: temple and imperial commemorative connection; distinguish Zheng He’s actions from later state construction of his memory.

Why he matters

His profile expands Buddhist history beyond the monastery. It shows how temples could participate in maritime diplomacy, imperial ceremony and the representation of Nanjing as a port and capital.

Research starting point: Nanjing Local Chronicles Office, Zheng He research visit.

Jiran · 寂然

Jiran represents a modern form of religious protection. During the crisis of 1937, accounts of Qixia Temple remember him as a monk who opened the temple to civilians seeking refuge.

Evidence level: modern institutional memory requiring careful comparison of temple records, wartime testimony and later public histories.

Why he matters

His profile shows that a temple’s historical role is not limited to ritual or architecture. In a period of crisis, the institution could also function as a place of shelter, moral action and historical memory.

Research starting point: Jiangsu report on Qixia’s wartime memory.

How to read these profiles

Each entry separates documented events, later traditions and unresolved questions. The figures are not presented as isolated biographies: follow the links to see how a person’s actions appear in inscriptions, temple locations, institutional changes and later memories.
