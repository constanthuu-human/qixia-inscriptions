The Site

A southern landscape of belief and authority.

Carved at Mount She near Jiankang, the Qixia grottoes emerged from Southern Dynasties aristocratic Buddhism. Their inscriptions record devotion, family memory, monastic expansion, and the political use of Buddhist patronage.

Chinese Text

朕闻锺山玉阙，羽驾之所巡游；昆岳金台，衣之所翔集。虽复真宗窅眇，神理希微，犹居三界之中，未出九天之外。唯有乘如广运，妙觉圆明，因无生以济有生，就无象而成大象。道隔去来之际，筌系靡得其端，理忘动寂之机，随迎罕窥其奥。得其门者，如髻宝之希逢；臻其极者，似昙花之难遇。

南齐征君明僧绍者，平原人也。仲雍诞其绵允，井伯播其灵苗，芳源肇于孟明，因即以明为姓。曾祖忱，晋著作郎。祖玩，晋建威将军。凤经流誉，雅韵隆于八儒；豹略申威，香名高于七校。父略，宋平原太守中书侍郎。朱明出抚，扬惠化而傍霑；紫诰攸司，罄忠规而奉上。征君早植净因，宿苞种智。悟真空于绮岁，体法性于青襟。照与神通，心将道合。遗荣轩冕，少无尘杂之情；讬志林岩，自叶幽贞之趣。亭亭秀气，掩璧月而架丹霄；皎皎清衿，漱琼湍而凌碧濑。即相非相，指万象为虚空；无我无人，等四流于寂灭。加以学穷儒肆，该综典坟，论极元津，精通《老》、《易》。至若鹿野龙宫之秘，猿江鹤树之文，莫不递贯清衷，总持丹府。班荆坐樾，独神王于亭皋；朗啸长吟，乃情超于宇宙。蒲轮每至，攀桂之节逾高；玉帛屡陈，枕石之诚弥固。遂乃缅怀飞遁，抗迹崂山；托岫疏阶，凭林结栋。纫兰制芰，方轻藻火之衣；爽籁风松，自代管弦之响。横经者四集，请益者千馀，高凤愧以韬光，张超谢其成市。于时南风不竞，东土构屯，人厌豺狼之毒，家充虵豕之铒。盗仍有道，望境归仁，共结盟誓之言。不犯征君之界，岂非至诚攸感，木石开心者乎。及元历告终，青光启祚，齐高祖希风伫德，侧席傍求，屡下征书，确乎不拔。其后又移居郁洲掩榆山栖云精舍，情亲鱼鸟，志狎烟霞，蜕影樊笼，萧然独往。齐建元元年，又下诏征为散骑侍郎，又不就。既而济岱沦胥，公私荡覆，稽天之浸，将湮蹈海之居；燎原之火，欲烬藏山之璞。乃鸿骞凤举，腾万仞以高翔，择木选君，相九土而遥集。凌江迥憩，遂届南京。负杖泉邱，游睨林壑，历观胜境，行次摄山，神谷仙岩，特符心赏。夜间披榛草，定迹深栖，树槿疏池，有终焉之志。此山其状如伞，故亦号曰伞山。丹穴红泉，共星河而竞泻，珠林镜𪩘，与月桂而交辉。鸟哢严虚，猿吟涧静。松门杳霭。去来千里之云，花楦丰茸，含吐十枝之日。实息心之胜地，乃宴坐之名区。爰集法流，于焉讲肄。音容秀澈，宇量端凝，投论会奇，兴言入妙。若洪锺之虚受，有击必扬；似明镜之忘疲，无来不应。于时元儒兼阐，道俗同归，俱号净名，以旌至德。先是山多猛噬，人罕登临。昇岩有仙谷之危，越涧等凭河之险。征君心不忤物，总万类以敷仁，故使物乃革心，屏三毒而归惠。兴风敛暴，遽承探鲠之恩；游雾含辛，自埒报珠之感。于时齐道方修，寤寐求贤。永明元年，又征为国子博士。征君隐居求志，义越乎由光；不降凝心，迹高于园绮。凿坏贞遁，漱石忘归，鹤版载临，豹姿逾远。俄有法师僧辩，承风景慕，翼徒振锡，翻然戾止。法师业隆三藏，道迈四依，戒行坚明，律轨严净。欣然一遇，叶契干龄。子琴为莫逆之交，温雪岂容声之友。因即邻岩构宇，别起梵居。耸峤飞柯，含风吐雾，栖霞之寺，由此创名。

福地裁基，肇发初心之誓；法门构邃，遽锺后说之辰。安居顷之，辩师迁化。六年顶拜，虽开青石之坛；千日威光，未建紫金之岳。征君积缘登妙，至感入微。尝梦法身，冠于曾𪩘，后因垂眺，屣步林亭。乃有浮磬吟空，写圆音于帷树；飞香散迥，腾宝气于炉峰。又睹真颜于岩之首，神光骇瞩，若登灵鹫之山；妙力难思，如游瞽龙之邑。岂止无垢佛国，独荫珠云；净德王家，方承珂雪。是知不行而至，冥通应感之符；为法而来，实昭光启之福。非夫慧因宿植，其孰与于此哉！于是拜受嘉征，愿言经始，将于岩壁，造大尊仪。乃眷为山，未遑初篑。遽而西州智士，与晓岳而俱倾；东国高人，随夜星而共没。琼瑶落彩，峰岫沈晖。永明二年，奄迁舟壑。第二子临沂公仲璋，顾慕曾峦，既崩心于岵望；徘徊曩构，更泣血于楹书。遂琢彼翠屏，爰开叶座，舍兹碧题，式建花宫。上宪优填之区，仰镂能仁之像；校美何充之宅，遽兴崇德之闱。逖彼萧宗，大宏释典，文惠太子及竟陵王，或澄少海之源派，朝宗于法海；或茂本枝之颖发，萌柢于禅枝。咸舍净财，光隆慧业。时有沙门法度，为智殿之栋梁，即此旧基，更兴新制。又造尊像十有馀龛，及梁运载兴，锐心回向，大林精舍，并事庄严。临川王载剖竹符，宣化惟扬之境；言寻奈苑，兴想拔茅之义。以天监一十五载造无量寿像一区，带地连光，合高五丈。满月之瑞，湛珠镜以出云崖；聚日之辉，昇璧轮而皎烟路。参差四注，周以鸟翅之房；迢遰千寻，饰以鱼鳞之瓦。击鸣干于爽籁，则步影齐归；丽停午于高曦，则息心攸萃。逾锦城而特建，掩银界而孤标。良由积慧所符，大士著甚深之业，用能遥诚克果，永代增希有之缘。以旷劫之隆因，开舍生之至福。伟哉壮观，无得而称。

朕肃纂祯图，丕承宝历，澄九溟而有截，宴八表而无为。紫塞丹岑，接封畿于上苑；白门青野，款賝贽于仙闱。将使率土苍生，镇昇仁寿之域，普天黔首，永蹈淳古之源。崇庆越于两仪，景运逾于万劫。属以冕旒多暇，物色傍求，瞻江海而载怀，咏林泉而兴想。钦风味道，恨不同时。古往今来，抚运化而虽寂，德崇业著，眷神理而犹存。寤寐遗尘，有兼前烈，瞻言胜轨，叹伫唯深。今故于彼度人，常满七七，各兼衣钵钱二百贯，绢二百匹，苏参拾斛，绣像织成像新旧翻译一切经一藏，并幡华等物。凭幽寻之曩迹，光显德门；托嘉遁之名区，追崇仁里。就福宇而延福，即祥基以缔祥。冀缘金囿之庭，近叶珠囊之耀，所愿通因法岸，契果禅林，九鼎与元极同安，七庙与紫微齐固。总三千之净土，并沐薰歌；罄百亿之恒沙，长为寿算；铁围之所苞括，玉烛之所照临，常餐六气之和，俱藻一音之听。夫象以尽意，意非象而不申；言以会情，情非言而不畅。是以发挥二谛，宏演四依，迥讬莲花之峰，遥刻芝英之字，庶海桑频变，孤超弇岳之碑，城芥屡空，独跨稽岑之篆。式陈茂实，乃作铭云：

悠悠法界，总总含生。轮回欲海，起灭身城。俱安大夜，共习无明。爱尘岳聚，毒树云平。（其一）

邈矣遍知，超然独悟。遽乘五演，高被六度。大空善说，中天巧谕。引彼迷途，归之觉路。（其二）

猗欤净行，育彩昆田。家承珪组，代著忠贤。戒支宿习，种智斯圆。栋梁三宝，薰修四禅。（其三）

爰始筮宾，薜萝攸整。蹈海沈迹，栖岩灭影。天地构屯，干戈互警。北林罔庇，南辕载骋。（其四）

翻飞泽国，历考山图。言瞻碧磴，自韫元珠。凭峰架室，枕壑通衢。鳣庭广跨，马帐宏敷。（其五）

同气相求，善邻遥讬。道符久敬，心均常乐。对辟金园，并疏银阁。谷停帝马，峦归梵鹤。（其六）

空分瑞塔，地积香台。珂月霄英，珠云旦来。千光雾起，七净霞闻。谷边飞锡，涧下乘杯。（其七）

桂𪩘参差，松亭隐霭。石坛照锦，瑶泉泻籁。岫接香炉，峰承宝盖。翔凫演法，毒龙销害。（其八）

梵宫既启，福海长深。噬虺忘穴，飞鸮革音。群生普戴，奕祀同钦。不有高节，宁符宿心。（其九）

御扆多闲，闻风逖想。茂轨遐劭，清晖遽往。伫契业于圆明，冀崇缘于方广。镂飞篆于曾岳，齐胜基于穹壤。（其十）

English Translation

I have heard that the jade towers of Mount Zhong were places through which the feathered carriages of immortals roamed, and that the golden terraces of Mount Kun were places where the feather-robed transcendents gathered. Yet although the true doctrine of the immortals is remote and obscure, and its divine principles subtle and faint, they still remain within the Three Realms and have not passed beyond the Nine Heavens.

Only the Vehicle of Thusness extends without limit, while wondrous enlightenment is perfect and radiant. Relying upon the principle of non-arising, it delivers those who possess life; taking the formless as its basis, it brings forth the Great Image. Its Way transcends the boundary between coming and going, so no verbal device can grasp its origin. Its principle leaves behind the workings of movement and stillness, so neither pursuit nor reception can disclose its depths. Those who find its gate are as rare as those who encounter the jewel concealed in a royal topknot; those who reach its ultimate end are as uncommon as the blossoming of the uḍumbara flower.

During the Southern Qi dynasty there lived the summoned recluse Ming Sengshao, a native of Pingyuan. Zhongyong engendered his long line of descendants; Jingbo spread its numinous shoots; and the fragrant source of the family began with Mengming, from whom the clan took Ming as its surname.

His great-grandfather Chen served under the Jin dynasty as a Gentleman in the Bureau of Historiography. His grandfather Wan served under the Jin as General Who Establishes Might. The phoenix-like refinement of their learning spread their reputation, and their elegant character stood eminent among the Confucian scholars; their leopard-like military strategy displayed its power, and their fragrant fame rose above the officers of the military academies.

His father Lüe served under the Liu Song dynasty as Administrator of Pingyuan and Palace Secretariat Attendant. When sent out to govern, he spread benevolent instruction that benefited all around him; when entrusted with the imperial decrees, he exhausted his loyal counsel in service to the sovereign.

From an early age, the Summoned Gentleman planted pure causes and carried within himself the seeds of wisdom accumulated in former lives. In the years of patterned youth, he awakened to true emptiness; while still wearing the blue collar of a student, he understood the nature of the Dharma. His insight communicated with the numinous, and his mind accorded with the Way.

He cast aside the honors of carriages and official caps, having never in youth possessed feelings contaminated by worldly dust. He entrusted his aspirations to forests and cliffs, naturally conforming to the secluded integrity of the recluse. His lofty and exceptional vitality eclipsed the jade-like moon and rose into the crimson heavens; his brilliant purity washed itself in jeweled torrents and soared above emerald rapids.

Within appearances he perceived non-appearance and pointed to the ten thousand phenomena as emptiness. Recognizing neither self nor person, he regarded the four currents of worldly existence as equal in quiescent extinction.

Moreover, he exhausted the full range of Confucian learning and comprehensively mastered the ancient canons. His discussions reached the ultimate ford of profound mystery, and he thoroughly understood the Laozi and the Book of Changes. As for the secrets of the Deer Park and the Dragon Palace, and the writings associated with the Ape River and the Crane Trees, there was nothing that did not pass successively through his lucid mind and become completely retained within his inner treasury.

Spreading brushwood as a mat and sitting beneath the shade of trees, his spirit alone reigned supreme over the open countryside. When he gave a clear whistle or prolonged chant, his feelings passed beyond the universe. Imperial carriages with wheels wrapped in rushes repeatedly came to summon him, yet his integrity as one who grasped the cassia branch of reclusion only rose higher. Gifts of jade and silk were repeatedly presented, yet his sincerity in resting his head upon the rocks became still firmer.

He therefore turned his thoughts toward distant withdrawal and raised his traces above the world on Mount Lao. Relying upon a mountain recess, he cut steps into the slope; supported by the forest, he joined timbers to construct a dwelling. He threaded orchids and fashioned garments from water plants, regarding the ceremonial robes ornamented with algae and flames as things of little worth. The clear sounds of wind passing through the pines replaced for him the music of flutes and strings.

Those who came carrying the classics gathered from all directions, and more than a thousand came to request instruction. Even Gao Feng would have been ashamed to conceal his brilliance before him, while Zhang Chao would have yielded to the crowds that made the place resemble a marketplace.

At that time, the power of the south was failing and the eastern lands were thrown into disorder. The people were sickened by the cruelty of jackals and wolves, and households became prey for serpents and wild boars. Yet even the brigands retained some sense of the Way. Looking toward the borders of his domain, they submitted to his benevolence and together swore an oath never to violate the territory of the Summoned Gentleman. Was this not because his perfect sincerity produced a response so profound that even wood and stone would have opened their hearts?

When the former calendar came to an end and the azure radiance of the Qi dynasty inaugurated its rule, Emperor Gaozu of Qi admired his character and waited upon his virtue. Leaving the seat beside him vacant, the emperor sought him in every direction and repeatedly issued summonses, but he remained firmly unmovable.

He later moved to the Qiyun Monastery on Yanyu Mountain in Yuzhou. He treated fish and birds as intimate companions and took mist and clouds as his familiar friends. Having shed every trace of the confining cage, he went his solitary way in complete freedom.

In the first year of the Jianyuan era, an edict once more summoned him to serve as a Cavalier Attendant, but again he did not accept the appointment.

Before long, the territories around the Ji River and Mount Tai were together engulfed, and both public and private property were swept away. Floodwaters reaching the heavens were about to submerge his dwelling beside the sea, while flames spreading across the plains threatened to consume the jade concealed within the mountain. He therefore rose like a great wild goose and lifted himself like a phoenix, soaring ten thousand fathoms into the sky. Like a bird selecting its tree and a worthy man choosing his ruler, he surveyed the Nine Regions from afar.

Crossing the great river, he rested at a distant place and eventually arrived at the southern capital. Carrying his staff among springs and hills, he wandered through forests and valleys and inspected the finest landscapes. During his travels he came to Mount She. Its divine valleys and immortal cliffs corresponded especially well with what delighted his heart.

He cleared away brambles and grass, established his traces in deep seclusion, planted hibiscus trees, and opened a pond, intending to remain there until the end of his life. Because the mountain was shaped like an umbrella, it was also called Umbrella Mountain.

Crimson caverns and red springs poured forth in competition with the River of Stars. Pearl-like forests and mirrorlike crags shone together with the cassia tree of the moon. Birds sang among the empty cliffs, and gibbons called through the silent ravines. The gateway of pines disappeared into the distant haze, receiving clouds that had traveled a thousand li. Luxuriant flowering trees seemed to swallow and release the ten-branched sun.

It was truly an excellent place for calming the mind and a celebrated region for seated meditation. There he gathered those who followed the stream of the Dharma and instructed them in Buddhist learning.

His voice and appearance were refined and lucid, while his bearing was upright and composed. When arguments were presented to him, he grasped their extraordinary points; whenever he spoke, his words entered the wondrous. He was like a great bell whose emptiness enables it to receive every blow and resound whenever struck. He was like a bright mirror that never tires, responding to everything that comes before it.

At that time, he expounded both the teachings of profound mystery and the learning of the Confucians. Clerics and laypeople alike returned to him, and all called him Vimalakīrti in recognition of his supreme virtue.

Previously, the mountain had contained many savage beasts, and few people dared to climb it. Ascending its cliffs involved the dangers of an immortal valley, while crossing its ravines was as perilous as relying upon the River of Heaven.

The Summoned Gentleman’s heart was never hostile toward any creature. He embraced the ten thousand species and extended benevolence to all. Consequently, even the beasts transformed their hearts, put aside the Three Poisons, and submitted to his kindness. Those that raised the wind restrained their violence, as though remembering the favor of having an obstruction removed from their throats; those that wandered through the mist endured hardship, as though repaying a benefactor with a pearl.

At that time, the Way of Qi was flourishing, and the court sought worthy men both waking and sleeping. In the first year of the Yongming era, he was again summoned to serve as Erudite of the Directorate of Education.

The Summoned Gentleman, however, remained in seclusion in order to preserve his purpose. In righteousness he surpassed Xu You and Wu Guang; in his refusal to submit and in his concentration of mind, his conduct rose above that of the recluses Dongyuan Gong and Qili Ji. Maintaining the pure withdrawal of one who would break through a wall to escape worldly service, and rinsing stones until he forgot to return, he received another summons inscribed upon the crane tablet, yet his leopard-like figure withdrew still farther away.

Not long afterward, the Dharma master Sengbian heard of his reputation and admired his example. Leading his disciples and raising his monk’s staff, he traveled swiftly and arrived there.

The Dharma master’s mastery of the Tripiṭaka was profound, his attainment in the Way surpassed the Four Reliances, his observance of the precepts was firm and radiant, and his disciplinary practice was strict and pure. The two rejoiced upon meeting, and their agreement seemed to have been formed across a thousand years. They became friends of perfect mutual understanding, a friendship that even the celebrated companions of antiquity could not have surpassed.

Sengbian therefore constructed buildings beside the neighboring cliffs and separately founded a Buddhist monastery. Its buildings rose over the peaks, while its projecting eaves seemed to fly; it contained the wind and breathed forth mist. From this foundation the Temple of Qixia received its name.

The foundation of this blessed ground had only just been laid, giving initial expression to a long-held vow, and the profound gateway of the Dharma had only just been constructed when the hour of the master’s final teaching suddenly arrived. After residing there only a short time, Master Bian passed into transformation.

Although the blue-stone altar had been opened for years of prostration, and a thousand days of awe-inspiring radiance had passed, the purple-gold mountain of a great Buddha image had not yet been erected.

The Summoned Gentleman had accumulated conditions that led toward the wondrous, and his deepest sincerity penetrated the most subtle realm. He once dreamed that the Dharma body of the Buddha towered above a lofty peak.

Later, while looking into the distance, he walked in light shoes through a forest pavilion. Suddenly a floating stone chime sounded in the empty air, spreading the Buddha’s perfect voice among the curtain-like trees. Fragrance flew outward and dispersed into the distance, while a precious vapor rose over the incense-burner-shaped peak.

He then saw the true countenance of the Buddha above the summit of the cliff. Divine light dazzled his eyes. It was as though he had ascended Vulture Peak; its wondrous power was inconceivable, as though he had traveled through the city of the blind dragons.

How could this have been only the jeweled cloud that shaded the stainless Buddha-land, or merely the white-jade snow received by the household of King Pure Virtue? From this we know that to arrive without traveling was a sign of the hidden communication between stimulus and response; to come for the sake of the Dharma truly revealed the blessing that would inaugurate its radiance. Had he not planted the causes of wisdom in former lives, how could he have participated in such a manifestation?

He therefore bowed and accepted the auspicious sign. He declared his vow to begin the undertaking and intended to carve a great sacred image into the cliff face.

Yet although he turned his concern toward the mountain, he had not had time even to raise the first basket of earth when the wise man of the western region fell together with the mountain of dawn, and the lofty man of the eastern lands disappeared together with the stars of night. Jewels lost their brilliance, and the peaks and ridges sank into darkness.

In the second year of the Yongming era, he suddenly passed away, like a boat secretly removed from a ravine.

His second son, Zhongzhang, Duke of Linyi, looked toward the lofty ridges with grief and longing. His heart broke as he gazed toward the paternal hill, and as he wandered among the structures formerly planned by his father, he wept tears of blood before the writings upon the pillars.

He therefore carved into the emerald screen of rock and opened a lotus throne. Giving up his green-gabled residence, he established a palace of flowers. Above, he took as his model the sanctuary in which King Udayana had fashioned the first image of the Buddha, and reverently carved an image of the Able and Benevolent One, Śākyamuni. Rivaling the beauty of the residence donated by He Chong, he immediately raised a sanctuary devoted to exalted virtue.

The distant descendants of the imperial house of Xiao greatly promoted the Buddhist scriptures. Crown Prince Wenhui and the Prince of Jingling either clarified the streams flowing from the Lesser Sea and directed them toward the ocean of the Dharma, or caused the flourishing branches of the imperial house to send their roots into the tree of meditation. All donated pure wealth and gloriously enlarged the works of wisdom.

At that time, the monk Fadu served as a pillar and roof beam of the palace of wisdom. Upon the existing foundation he created a new design and constructed more than ten niches containing sacred images.

When the fortune of the Liang dynasty arose, its rulers devoted themselves earnestly to the turning of the mind toward enlightenment, and the great woodland monastery was adorned in every respect.

The Prince of Linchuan then received the bamboo tally of regional authority and spread transforming instruction through the lands of Yangzhou. Seeking the mango grove of the Buddha, he conceived the shared purpose symbolized by the pulling of joined grass roots.

In the fifteenth year of the Tianjian era, he constructed an image of Amitāyus. Connected with the earth and joined with radiance, it reached a total height of five zhang.

The auspicious countenance of the full moon emerged from the cloudy cliff like a clear pearl mirror. The gathered radiance of many suns rose like a jade disk and illuminated the road through the mist.

Unevenly arranged structures extended in four directions, surrounding the image with chambers like the wings of birds. Rising into the distance for what seemed a thousand fathoms, they were ornamented with roof tiles like the scales of fish.

When the wooden striker sounded through the clear air, the shadows of approaching footsteps returned together. When the brilliant sun stood at the height of noon, those who sought to calm their minds gathered there.

It surpassed the distinguished monuments of Brocade City and stood alone, outshining the Silver Realm. This was truly because accumulated wisdom had produced a corresponding sign, and because the Great Being had established a most profound work. Through this, distant sincerity succeeded in bearing fruit, and rare conditions were increased for generations to come.

By means of exalted causes accumulated through immeasurable kalpas, it opened the supreme merit of relinquishing one’s own life. Magnificent indeed was this great spectacle: no words could adequately describe it.

I have reverently succeeded to the auspicious imperial inheritance and received the precious calendar of rule. I have brought clarity to the Nine Seas and united their people, and I govern the Eight Regions peacefully through non-action.

Purple passes and crimson peaks connect the imperial territory with the upper gardens. The White Gate and green fields welcome those who present treasures and tribute before the immortal palace.

I intend that all living people throughout the realm shall continually ascend into the region of benevolence and long life, and that all the black-haired people beneath Heaven shall forever walk within the source of ancient simplicity. May this exalted blessing surpass Heaven and Earth, and may this splendid fortune extend beyond ten thousand kalpas.

As the duties associated with the imperial crown have allowed me some leisure, I have searched in every direction for worthy men. Looking toward the rivers and seas, I have cherished thoughts of them; chanting of forests and springs, I have conceived a longing for the recluse.

I revere his character and savor his Way, regretting that I did not live in his time. Although antiquity has passed and the present has come, and although, following the transformations of destiny, he has entered stillness, his virtue was exalted, his work was manifest, and in contemplating the divine principle one finds that he still survives.

Waking and sleeping, I have thought of the traces he left behind, which stand together with the achievements of the worthy men of former generations. Looking toward his victorious example, I have remained in profound admiration.

I therefore command that forty-nine persons regularly be ordained at that place. I additionally grant robes and bowls, two hundred strings of cash, two hundred bolts of silk, thirty hu of clarified butter, embroidered images, woven images, one complete collection of all scriptures in both the old and new translations, together with banners, flowers, and other objects.

Relying upon the former traces found in this secluded place, I seek to make his virtuous lineage shine. Entrusting these gifts to the celebrated region of his noble withdrawal, I honor the village of his benevolence.

At this sanctuary of merit, I extend merit; upon this auspicious foundation, I establish further auspiciousness. I hope that, through the garden of gold, we may approach the radiance of the jeweled repository of scripture. I pray that we may pass through the causes leading to the shore of the Dharma and accord with their fruits in the forest of meditation.

May the Nine Tripods of the state remain secure together with the Supreme Pole, and may the Seven Ancestral Temples remain firm together with the Purple Tenuity Palace of Heaven.

May all the pure lands of the three-thousandfold great-thousand world together receive the blessings of fragrant hymns. May all the sands of the Ganges throughout the hundred billion worlds forever be added to the measure of the imperial lifespan.

All that is enclosed by the Iron Encircling Mountains and illuminated by the harmonious Jade Candle shall continually partake of the harmony of the Six Vital Forces and together receive the teaching of the Buddha’s single voice.

Images are used to exhaust meaning, yet without images meaning cannot be displayed. Words are used to communicate feeling, yet without words feeling cannot be expressed.

Therefore, in order to illuminate the Two Truths and broadly expound the Four Reliances, I entrust this text to the distant Lotus-Blossom Peak and carve these numinous words upon it.

May it stand alone above the stele of Mount Yan even after seas have repeatedly become mulberry fields; may its seal-script inscription surpass that of the peaks of Kuaiji even after cities filled with mustard seeds have repeatedly been emptied.

Interpretation

Imperial history written into stone.

The inscription should be interpreted not as a neutral biography, but as a Tang imperial reconstruction of Qixia Temple’s past. It presents Ming Sengshao’s refusal of office as moral purity, then absorbs that independent reputation into imperial ideology: the ideal ruler proves his virtue by recognizing and honoring worthy recluses. It also shows that Qixia Temple was created through cooperation among aristocratic families, monks, and royal patrons rather than by one founder alone. Finally, the inscription connects Buddhist merit with political order, dynastic stability, and imperial longevity. Its central message is that supporting Buddhism could strengthen both the moral authority of the emperor and the legitimacy of the state.

Historical Background

From a recluse’s retreat to a dynastic monument.

This inscription is the Ming Zhengjun Stele of Qixia Temple, erected under Tang emperor Gaozong in 676 CE. However, it describes events beginning nearly two centuries earlier, during the Southern Qi and Liang dynasties.

Ming Sengshao was an aristocratic scholar and Buddhist recluse who repeatedly refused government appointments. After warfare forced him to leave northern China, he settled at Mount She near Jiankang, modern Nanjing. There he established a retreat and attracted monks, scholars, and students. The monk Sengbian later founded a monastery beside his residence, creating the early Qixia Temple.

Ming planned to carve a large Buddhist image into the mountain but died in 484 before completing it. His son Ming Zhongzhang, the monk Fadu, and members of the Southern Qi and Liang royal families continued the project, producing the earliest Qixia grottoes.

The Tang court later erected this stele to honor Ming Sengshao, support Qixia Temple, and connect Buddhist patronage with imperial legitimacy, social order, and the protection of the dynasty.

English Translation

People say that Qixia is one of the four great monasteries within the realm because eminent monks have appeared there generation after generation. From the masters known as the Elder and Younger Lang during the Qi and Liang dynasties down to the present master, their reputations followed one another. Therefore, throughout the region south of the Yangtze, people respectfully called him the Master of Qixia.

The Master’s Dharma name was Tanbi, and his secular surname was Wang. He was a descendant of Duke Wenxian of Langya under the Jin dynasty. After his family migrated south during the Yongjia period, they settled in Juqu. His grandfather, Wang Shiqian, served as Administrator of Kuaiji. His father, Wang Zhi, refused official employment and was known locally as a recluse.

From infancy, the Master showed no interest in ordinary childhood games. As soon as he could speak, his intelligence became apparent. Whenever he heard difficult teachings, he immediately understood their deepest meanings. When he grew older, he refused to eat meat and remarked, “Perhaps Heaven intends to cleanse me as a vessel.”

He shaved his head under Vinaya Master Xiyu in Jinling and received full ordination from the sea-crossing Master Jianzhen. Later, together with his friend Vinaya Master En of Gaoling, he followed the example of the earlier monks Huiyuan and Huiyong and withdrew to Donglin Temple on Mount Lu. Although he wished to escape fame, his reputation continued to grow.

Students came to him from the regions of Wu, Chu, Qing and Xu. After completing five monastic summers, he began lecturing on the Vinaya at Longxing Temple in Yuzhang. Students holding their scriptures crowded around him like reeds. The following year, he served at the ordination platform of Ming Temple. During the Zhide era, an imperial order formally assigned him to that monastery. He later presided repeatedly over ceremonies at the Ganlu ordination platform, displaying such solemn discipline that he became a model for the entire monastic community.

At the beginning of the Dali era, he returned to Qixia. He conducted fifteen ordination assemblies and delivered thirty-seven series of lectures on the scriptures and monastic discipline.

Governor Xiao of Lanling greatly admired his character and appointed him sengzheng, the official responsible for supervising the regional Buddhist community. Under Tanbi’s leadership, monastic discipline and administration were greatly strengthened, and his authority was accepted by all sections of the Buddhist community.

In the fourteenth year of the Dali era, he declared that although he had devoted himself to the Vinaya, he had not yet escaped the limitations that bind the hearer. He therefore investigated the teachings associated with Caoxi and Niutou. After prolonged meditation and reflection, he attained an inner understanding and said: “This is how a true man should comprehend the mind.”

In the first year of the Jianzhong era, he meditated in an empty valley and remained undisturbed by external phenomena. Later, followers at Waguan Temple invited him to reside there, describing him as the greatest Buddhist leader of Jiangnan. He accepted their invitation and left his mountain retreat.

On the sixth day of the eleventh month in the thirteenth year of the Zhenyuan era, he entered final stillness while seated in the Vinaya Hall of Waguan Temple. A stupa was later erected behind Xinting. He was seventy-five years old and had completed fifty-one monastic years.

Among his disciples were Mingze, Daoxing, Xingquan, Chengguan, Zhiman, Huixing, Lingjin, Changjing, Riyao and Huideng. All became guides for later students. Just as the greatness of Confucius was revealed through his distinguished disciples, the achievements of Qixia’s disciples revealed the greatness of their teacher.

Baocheng later led a major monastic community around Mount Lu. Just as Upagupta inherited the Dharma from Śāṇavāsa, Baocheng was presented as a leader of the Buddhist tradition. I, Liu Ke, had long enjoyed Baocheng’s friendship. He instructed me to compose this account for his master.

The inscription praises Tanbi as a descendant of the Wang clan of Langya, a Buddhist whose wisdom cut through obstruction, a master of discipline, and a teacher whose transmission continued like a flame passing from one piece of wood to another.

Interpretation

Monastic authority and religious transmission.

This inscription presents Qixia Temple as an institution whose importance came from a continuous succession of eminent monks. Tanbi’s authority is built through his aristocratic Wang ancestry, his ordination connection with Jianzhen, and the network of disciples who continued his teachings.

It also records an interaction between Vinaya and Chan. Tanbi did not reject discipline when he began studying Chan; Chan insight appears as a higher stage built upon his earlier mastery of monastic rules. Governor Xiao’s appointment of Tanbi as sengzheng shows official recognition of a monk who helped regulate the regional Buddhist community, but the stele does not describe direct imperial sponsorship. The disciple list turns Tanbi’s biography into evidence that Qixia was a center of ordination, education, and institutional leadership.

Historical Background

Qixia as a center of discipline and teaching.

Liu Ke composed the inscription in 830, thirty-three years after Tanbi’s death. The monk Yungao provided the calligraphy, while Qi Yu wrote the seal-script title; records state that the inscription was erected again in 854.

Tanbi lived during the middle Tang dynasty. Trained first in Vinaya, he received ordination from Jianzhen, taught and administered ordination platforms at several monasteries, and returned to Qixia during the early Dali period. There he organized ordinations, lectured on Buddhist texts, and was appointed regional sengzheng. Late in his career, he also studied the Chan traditions of Caoxi and Niutou.

English Translation

Sacred mountain and transformed landscape

It is said that Heaven contains divine palaces and the earth possesses numinous storehouses. Ancient writers recorded towers at crossroads and monasteries extending for a thousand paces; yet even peaks piled like steamers and ridges dense as halls are hardly sufficient to describe this place. Within Jiangcheng County of Langya Commandery in Southern Xuzhou stands Mount She. Because its form resembles an umbrella, it is also called Umbrella Mountain. An old account explains that the mountain contains many medicinal herbs useful for nourishing life, and therefore received the name “She,” meaning to preserve and nurture. To the south lay an old settlement and a fortified post; to the north stood an abandoned village and a diviner’s former residence. On the mountain’s southwestern corner there had been a non-Buddhist establishment, but epidemic disease soon destroyed it. The remaining teachings of the Three Pure Ones could not overcome the terrors of suffering; the Buddhist teaching of the ten thousand virtues then transformed the place into a realm of meditation.

Ming Sengshao’s retreat

There appeared the layman Ming Sengshao of Pingyuan, whose understanding of emptiness was profound and whose grasp of ultimate principle was lofty. He abandoned the honors of carriage and official cap and withdrew into cliffs and caves. During the Taishi era of the Liu Song, he visited this mountain and formed the intention of ending his life here. Villagers warned him that wolves, tigers, and poisonous snakes had long made the paths impassable. He replied: “Among all poisons, none exceeds the Three Poisons of greed, anger, and delusion. If one acts with loyalty and trust, one may tread even through water and fire; what can wild beasts do?” He cut timber, cleared grass, opened paths, removed brambles, and built a thatched dwelling. For more than twenty years he kept apart from ordinary society. Violence ceased around his crossing places, and no one broke into his chests; people said that these changes were responses to the force of his sincerity.

Fadu and the first monastery

The Chan master Fadu, whose family came from Huanglong, later arrived at the Buddhist community. His ascetic conduct was complete, his understanding of Dharma-nature pure, and he formed a profound friendship with Ming Sengshao. While he was lecturing on the Sutra of Infinite Life in the mountain residence, golden light suddenly illuminated the room at midnight, and within the light appeared forms resembling terraces and buildings. The received text then says that the layman gave up his residence with the intention of creating a monastery, which Fadu constructed on the third day of the first month in the seventh year of Yongming. The buildings were simple and rustic; the cliffs were steep, the ravine entrances secluded. Plants flourished, while clouds and mist supplied the landscape with changing color.

Vision, death, and continuation by the family

Ming Sengshao dreamed that the cliff shone with the radiance of the Tathagata and later believed that he glimpsed the same sign while awake. Such sacred responses, the inscription argues, were not remote: spiritual presence already inhabited the mountain. Yet before he could complete his plan, Ming died. His second son, Ming Zhongzhang, then serving as magistrate of Linyi, inherited the undertaking. Together with Fadu, he carved on the western cliff a seated Buddha of Infinite Life, accompanied by two bodhisattvas. The main image measured more than three zhang in height and rose to four zhang with its base. The carving was described as magnificent: lotus forms dazzled the eye, stone surfaces caught and darkened the light, and the faces and halos appeared to rival the sun and moon.

Miracles and aristocratic patronage

In the second year of Datong, light was said to shine from the top of the niche, turning the trees and cliffs red like a hall of fire. Fadu understood this as a sign of his approaching death and passed away at the temple. The inscription then names a broad network of patrons: the Southern Qi Crown Prince Wenhui, the princes of Yuzhang, Jingling, and Shi’an, a noblewoman associated with the Jiangxia princely house, the governor Tian Huan, and others. They contributed wealth and labor to carve images from the mountain. Under the Liang, the Prince of Linchuan opened his treasury to enlarge and decorate the site with color and metal ornament. Monks moved through the grasses chanting, and the architecture seemed to emerge directly from the mountain mist.

A public religious landscape

The temple was not presented as a private retreat alone. Princes, high officials, junior clerks, and common administrators all climbed through the forests, entered the monastery, and worshiped at its ritual spaces. The inscription claims that these visitors cleansed ignorance and the noise of worldly life. Qixia thus became a public moral landscape in which social groups of different rank could encounter Buddhist practice.

Huizhen and institutional expansion

The master Huizhen inherited Fadu’s responsibilities. He organized support, expanded the temple’s resources, and established lands and other institutional foundations. This passage shows that the monastery’s survival depended not only on miraculous visions or elite generosity but also on administration, property, and the orderly transfer of leadership.

Senglang and the Three Treatises

The celebrated master Senglang had left the region of the Liao River and sought the Buddhist Way near the capital. Distinguished in discipline and learning, he expounded the meaning of the Mahayana scriptures and the doctrine of the Middle Way. For almost thirty years he remained away from the imperial city. Emperor Wu of Liang repeatedly summoned him, but Senglang did not leave the mountain. In the eleventh year of Tianjian, the emperor therefore sent ten monks to Qixia to receive instruction in the central principles of the Three Treatises. The inscription presents this as evidence that the mountain monastery could possess intellectual authority strong enough to draw the court toward it.

Recluses, spirits, and sacred geography

The text also records the recluse Xiao Mi, who lived on the mountain for many years and asked to be buried beside Senglang. It then recounts a local spirit identified as Jin Shang, a minister of ancient Chu, who supposedly received Buddhist precepts from Fadu and later appeared wearing a bodhisattva headdress and monastic robe to request teaching. Whether read as belief, legend, or institutional memory, these stories convert the entire mountain into Buddhist sacred geography: recluses, political figures, monks, and local spirits all become part of the temple’s religious history.

Huibu and Jiang Zong

The master Huibu had left worldly life at an early age, maintained strict discipline, mastered the three vehicles, and served as a model for the Buddhist community. Jiang Zong states that he had admired Huibu for more than ten years and recently received the bodhisattva precepts from him at Mount She. The inscription is therefore not detached antiquarian writing. Its author openly places himself inside the temple’s religious lineage as a lay disciple seeking to repay his teacher.

Why carve the history in stone

Jiang concludes that words and intentions are difficult to preserve and that writing materials decay. Stone is more durable than brush and paper. He therefore records the temple’s miraculous responses, patrons, monks, and sacred landscape on the stele. The closing verse describes life as temporary, Buddhist teaching as a raft and boat, and the temple as a place where mountain paths, meditation halls, divine beings, and human visitors meet. The final invitation is direct: travelers should stop and remain.

Northern Song recut

The attached Northern Song colophon explains that the earlier stone had been destroyed during the Huichang suppression of Buddhism and later re-established, but was again broken and illegible. The monk Qixian purchased a new stone and had the text recopied and engraved on 17 March 1040. This colophon is essential: the continuous text now read online comes through a medieval act of restoration rather than directly from an untouched sixth-century monument.

Historical Background

From mountain retreat to Buddhist institution.

Jiang Zong composed the inscription in the Southern Chen, conventionally dated to 588. It looks back to the Liu Song, Southern Qi, and Liang periods, tracing Ming Sengshao’s retreat, Fadu’s monastic activity, the first monumental cliff images, and later expansion through elite patronage.

The text also presents Qixia as a doctrinal center. Emperor Wu of Liang repeatedly summoned Senglang; when the monk did not go to court, the emperor sent ten monks to Mount She to study the Three Treatises.

The original stone was lost. A Northern Song colophon records that the inscription was recut in 1040 after earlier destruction and damage. Excavated fragments of the Chen stone now require the received foundation narrative to be read cautiously.

Interpretation

Stone made an institutional past.

The inscription does not describe Qixia as the achievement of one founder. Recluses supplied sacred authority; monks taught and administered; families continued unfinished projects; princes and officials converted wealth into images, halls, land, and public access.

It also rewrites the mountain itself. Cliffs, medicinal plants, light, mist, dangerous animals, and local spirits are absorbed into a Buddhist sacred geography.

Jiang identifies Huibu as his precept master, placing a senior official inside Qixia’s religious lineage. The stele therefore joins personal devotion, literary authority, and institutional memory.

Archaeology complicates one major claim: the received 1040 text links Ming Sengshao’s residence directly to the monastery, while the excavated Chen fragments suggest the retreat and the earliest institutional temple may have occupied different locations.

English Translation

Qixia Temple possesses mountains and waters of unsurpassed beauty, and its landscape is extraordinary. The former residence of the recluse Ming Sengshao stands here, as the old stele written by Lord Jiang has already explained in detail. Emperor Gaozong also engraved his imperial composition upon enduring stone. His calligraphy shines like heavenly light throughout this secluded valley.

Mount She lies approximately fifty li from the capital. Its road was once broad enough for two carriages to travel side by side, and the journey could be completed within a morning. Later, however, warfare brought disorder to the Central Plain. Fortifications filled the outskirts, no herdboys remained in the fields, and travelers became lost among the branching paths.

When Emperor Yizu Wu was still living in this region before his rise, he visited the mountain in search of the Way and ordered the responsible officials to construct a new road. After the imperial patron had departed, however, the road gradually fell into disuse. The imperial carriage no longer returned, and the former route was abandoned. Yet the traces left by an honored ruler should not be allowed to disappear.

In the xinhai year of the Baoda era, the country was peaceful, the harvest was abundant, government affairs were light, and the people had time to undertake public work.

A monk of Qixia Temple named Daoyan was highly respected among the Buddhist clergy and constantly considered how he might benefit others. A local resident named Zhuang Sicong came from a wealthy family but was willing to distribute what he had accumulated.

They lamented the disappearance of the roadside stations and sympathized with the difficulties faced by travelers. Together, they cut away thorns and undergrowth, cleared pits and hollows, opened a broad and level road, and abandoned the indirect side paths.

They built a tall pavilion beside the road and constructed a substantial bridge across the stream. They dug a well of fresh water to relieve travelers suffering from heat and erected stone markers to guide those who had lost their way.

Trees, mist, and wind could now be viewed in every direction, while the mountain peaks and temple buildings appeared solemnly ahead. Through this work, the road through Jiangcheng once again displayed the order appropriate to the territory surrounding a capital.

My official responsibilities sometimes leave me with leisure, and I have visited this mountain repeatedly. I am pleased that travelers can now follow a direct road, and I admire the persistence of these two elders.

I have therefore composed this text and ordered it to be carved in stone to commemorate the completion of their work. May later public-spirited people reopen the route when necessary and repair it whenever it is damaged.

Even if this stone tablet eventually becomes worn, may the meaning of this text never disappear. Would that not be a worthy achievement?

Recorded by Xu Xuan, Vice Director in the Ministry of War and Drafter of Imperial Proclamations, on the first day of the eighth month of that year.

Interpretation

A road as moral and political order.

The first road was ordered by a ruler, but its restoration depended upon a monk and a wealthy local donor. Xu Xuan then preserved their achievement in an official inscription. Qixia’s accessibility therefore joined political authority, monastic initiative, and private patronage.

The bridge, well, pavilion, and direction markers served ordinary travelers as well as worshippers, turning Buddhist compassion into practical public service. By claiming that the road restored the “order of the royal domain,” the inscription also made safe movement around the capital a sign of effective government. Its final appeal for continuing repairs presented infrastructure maintenance as an ongoing public responsibility.

Historical Background

Qixia within the Southern Tang capital region.

Xu Xuan wrote this inscription in 951, when Jinling—modern Nanjing—was the Southern Tang capital. Qixia Temple stood about fifty li outside the city and relied on a usable road for monks, worshippers, officials, and other travelers.

An earlier road had been commissioned by Xu Wen, later honored by the Southern Tang court as Emperor Yizu Wu. After the route deteriorated, the monk Daoyan and the local donor Zhuang Sicong organized a straighter road, bridge, roadside pavilion, well, and stone direction markers. Xu Xuan commemorated the project and urged later generations to maintain it.

《立舍利塔诏》 · 隋文帝杨坚

English Translation

To the Secretariat:

Reverently contemplating the Perfectly Enlightened One, who possesses great compassion and mercy, protects all living beings, and serves as a bridge of salvation for every class of creature:

I have taken refuge in the Three Jewels and restored the sacred teaching. I wish that all the people within the Four Seas may join together in awakening the aspiration for enlightenment and cultivating meritorious deeds. May those living at present, as well as those of future generations, forever establish good causes and together attain the wondrous result.

Thirty monks shall be selected. They must understand Buddhist doctrine and be capable of explaining and spreading the teaching. Each monk shall be accompanied by two monastic attendants and one government official. They shall be provided with frankincense, horses, and the Buddha’s relics, and shall travel by separate routes to construct stupas in the designated prefectures.

Where no specific monastery has been named, the stupa shall be constructed at a monastery located among suitable mountains and waters. Where the designated place has no existing monastery, a peaceful monastery within the prefecture shall be chosen. The responsible government office shall produce a standard design and send it to each prefecture.

In places with large monastic communities, 360 monks shall participate; in the next category, 240; and in the next, 120. Where only a small number of monks are present, all available monks shall perform seven days of ritual walking and repentance.

These rites shall be conducted on behalf of myself, the empress, Crown Prince Guang, the other princes and descendants, officials inside and outside the court, all common people, and all visible and invisible living beings.

On the day when the ceremonies begin, the ritual signal shall be sounded. People may make donations regardless of whether they come from the same prefecture or another prefecture. Each person’s donation must not exceed ten cash.

The donated money shall be used to construct the stupa. If the donations are insufficient, the labor of registered adult men and materials from government storehouses may be used.

Monks and nuns throughout all the prefectures of the empire shall hold religious feasts in honor of the relics. At noon on the fifteenth day of the tenth month, the relics shall be placed into stone containers at the same time.

All officials from the commanders and governors down to the county commandants shall suspend military affairs and ordinary administrative work for seven days. They shall devote themselves to supervising the rituals and related activities, carrying them out with complete sincerity and reverence in accordance with my intentions.

The responsible officials shall implement this order.

Interpretation

Relics as instruments of imperial integration.

The edict turned a Buddhist ritual into a precisely organized state program. It specified the monks, routes, locations, designs, funding, officials, ceremonies, and exact time of deposition. At noon on one day, distant prefectures were joined under a single command, making the newly reunified Sui realm visible as one religious and political community.

Existing monasteries became an imperial network of construction sites, ritual centers, and links between the court and local society. Standard designs represented central authority, while the ordered list of beneficiaries placed Buddhist merit within the hierarchy of emperor, imperial family, officials, common people, and all beings. Popular donations were limited, but compulsory labor and government materials could complete the work: religious participation and state power operated together.

The edict supplies this administrative structure, but it does not itself command political loyalty. Stronger loyalty language appears in the accompanying Record of Responses to the Relics. The distinction keeps the political interpretation grounded in what this document actually says.

Historical Background

A Buddhist program for a reunited empire.

Emperor Wen issued the edict in 601, twelve years after the Sui defeated the Chen and reunified northern and southern China. It launched the first of three major relic-distribution campaigns in 601, 602, and 604, which created more than one hundred stupas across the empire.

Thirty qualified monks carried relics along separate routes with attendants and government representatives. Prefectural administrations, monasteries, local officials, residents, laborers, and state storehouses all participated. The relics were deposited simultaneously in stone containers at thirty prefectures, joining Buddhist restoration after earlier persecution with the administrative ambitions of the new dynasty.

English Translation

The Emperor Receives the Relics

Before the emperor ascended the throne, a Brahman monk visited his residence. The monk produced a bundle of relics and said: “You possess a charitable heart, so I leave these with you for veneration.” After the monk departed, no one was able to discover where he had gone.

Later, the emperor and the monk Tanqian each placed the relics in the palm of a hand and attempted to count them. Sometimes they appeared fewer and sometimes more, so their number could not be determined. Tanqian explained that the Buddha’s Dharma-body transcended numerical measurement and could not be calculated according to ordinary worldly principles. The emperor therefore commissioned a jeweled container in which the relics could be preserved.

The sacred nun Zhixian had previously told him: “The Buddhist teaching is about to disappear, and the divine beings have already departed toward the west. You will become the compassionate father of all beneath Heaven, restore Buddhism, and cause the divine beings to return.” The Northern Zhou later suppressed Buddhism. When the Sui dynasty received the imperial mandate, Buddhism was restored. The emperor frequently repeated Zhixian’s prediction and declared: “My rise occurred through the Buddha.” Images of Zhixian were therefore placed inside the relic pagodas established throughout the empire.

The Empire-Wide Pagoda Project

On the thirteenth day of the sixth month of the first year of the Renshou reign, the emperor stayed in the Renshou Hall of Renshou Palace. This was the anniversary of his birth. Each year on that date, he devoted himself to meritorious activities in order to repay the kindness of his parents.

He invited eminent monks to discuss the highest teaching and decided to select thirty elevated, peaceful, and pure locations throughout the prefectures of the empire. A relic pagoda would be established at each location.

The emperor personally brought out thirty relics in a seven-jeweled container. He placed them upon a table before the imperial seat and, together with the monks, burned incense and performed acts of reverence. He vowed to protect the Three Jewels through the true Dharma and deliver all living beings.

Thirty gold bottles and thirty glass containers were prepared. Each gold bottle was placed inside a glass container, and a relic was deposited within it. The thirty prefectures were ordered to lower the relics into copper and stone containers at noon on the fifteenth day of the tenth month, establishing the pagodas at the same moment.

Public Ceremony and Imperial Loyalty

The monks carried the relics toward the appointed monasteries. When they entered a prefecture, households were ordered to sweep and remove unclean objects. Monks, lay believers, men, and women came out from the cities to meet the relics, while commanders, governors, and other officials walked beside the procession.

The monks proclaimed that the emperor, motivated by the limitless compassion of a bodhisattva, had distributed the relics so that the entire empire could participate in the same virtuous cause. The population promised to abandon wrongdoing and vowed that, in life after life, they would continue to be subjects of the Great Sui.

Qixia Temple

In Jiangzhou, a pagoda was established at Qixia Temple. Before the relic arrived, a neighboring resident dreamed that the Buddha approached from the northwest. Jeweled canopies, banners, and flowers filled the monastery with radiance, while the assembled people carried flowers and incense to welcome him. When the relic later reached Qixia Temple, the event was said to have occurred exactly as it had appeared in the dream.

Interpretation

From local monastery to imperial sacred site.

The record presents Emperor Wen as both a Chinese sovereign and a Buddhist sacred ruler. Thirty relics, matching containers, coordinated officials, and one fixed moment of deposition joined distant monasteries in a single ritual system.

The vow to remain “subjects of the Great Sui” connects moral repentance, devotion to the Buddha, and political loyalty. Qixia consequently became more than the retreat of Ming Sengshao or a regional center of images and teaching: it became an official node in the sacred geography of a newly unified empire.

The reported miracles follow a recurring structure—imperial action, sacred response, confirmation of legitimate rule. They are historically valuable as evidence of how Sui authority was represented, not as independently verified proof that each supernatural event occurred.

Historical Background

Thirty relics, thirty prefectures, one ritual time.

In 601, Emperor Wen launched the first of three Renshou relic-distribution campaigns. The central court prepared containers, selected monks, involved provincial officials, organized local ceremonies, and ordered relics to be deposited simultaneously at thirty sites. Later campaigns followed in 602 and 604.

Qixia Temple was selected as the Jiangzhou site. Wang Shao’s brief passage records a dream said to anticipate the relic’s arrival. It proves that Qixia participated in the 601 project, but it does not establish that every part of the present stone pagoda dates from that year; the surviving architecture requires separate evidence.

English Translation

Buddhism and the Purpose of the Temple

Buddhist images entered China, and under the Han dynasty the name of White Horse Temple arose. The teaching of meditation came from the Western Regions, and under the Jin dynasty temples such as Qinglong became renowned.

Yet even the highest levels of existence remain temporary resting places, and even the Heaven of Neither Perception nor Non-perception may still be described as a burning house. Without Buddhist knowledge and patient endurance to rescue beings submerged in the long sleep of ignorance, how could the Three Obstructions and Six Objects of the Senses be cleared away so that the radiance of enlightenment might appear?

For this reason, a place of diligent Buddhist practice was established here, allowing the subtle and secret treasury of the Dharma to be revealed.

Ming Sengshao and the Early Temple

Qixia Temple began with the Southern Qi layman Ming Sengshao. He inherited a distinguished tradition, concealed his brilliance, and followed the Way in seclusion.

Imperial invitations reached his mountain dwelling, and gifts of jade and silk filled the roads leading to him. Yet he withdrew beyond the noise of the world and placed his aspirations among the highest peaks. His withdrawal recalled the recluses of antiquity, whose virtue subdued dangerous animals and whose lofty conduct attracted eminent companions.

Chan Master Fadu then arrived and formed a profound spiritual relationship with him. Fadu regularly lectured on the Sutra of Infinite Life. Auspicious light was said to illuminate the room, sometimes taking the form of banners and canopies, while mysterious music seemed to rise around the pillars like the ringing of a Dharma bell.

These events were remembered as signs that sacred beings and exemplary monks had gathered at the mountain.

The mountain possessed medicinal plants and was therefore associated with healing. Its shape resembled a great canopy or umbrella, while the temple appeared like a precious mountain illuminated by many suns or a full moon rising above the sea. Officials, commoners, monks, and lay believers consequently gathered there in great numbers. How magnificent it was!

Patronage under the Hongwu Emperor

When the Great Ancestor, the Hongwu Emperor, unified the empire, the mountain became part of the territory surrounding the imperial capital.

At the beginning of his reign, the emperor emphasized the importance of Buddhist causes and consequences. He restored the temple’s privileges, bestowed an official name upon it, repaired its buildings, and authorized the ordination of monks.

Through these measures, he established religious merit for future ages and supported Buddhist achievement at Qixia. It was hoped that people would escape spiritual entanglement and that every household would enjoy benevolence and long life.

Decline and the Jiajing Restoration

Before long, however, the temple’s former prosperity collapsed. Its monastic buildings fell silent, the sacred ground became covered by weeds, and its gilded images were damaged by wind and rain. A place that had once transmitted Buddhist teaching was reduced to a neglected ruin.

During the Jiajing era, officials including Zheng Xiao, Qian Bangyan, Hu Yongtong, He Liangjun, and He Liangfu took an interest in restoring the temple. They were widely educated and respected religious learning. They did not identify purity merely with external severity, nor truth simply with withdrawal into emptiness. Instead, they sought to understand the deeper meaning of Buddhist purity and truth.

They invited the monk Xingshan from Tianjie Temple to reside at Mount She and entrusted him with the restoration. Beginning under extremely difficult conditions, Xingshan reopened the obstructed source of Buddhist practice. Through compassionate conduct, he brought the light of understanding to those who remained confused.

At that time, the lecturer Zhenjie, the Chan monk Fahui, and other worthy monks also gathered at Qixia. Some traveled with their staffs to spread Buddhist doctrine, while others remained in the forests to pursue religious practice. Profound texts and difficult teachings were proclaimed like the sounding of a Dharma drum, and the light of wisdom traveled outward with the sound of Buddhist chanting.

The undergrowth had been removed, but the halls had not yet been fully reconstructed. The community remained too large for the limited buildings available.

Qingbai and the Patronage Network

Xingshan had intended to enlarge the monastery, but he was appointed to an office within the Buddhist administrative system and therefore left Qixia. He was succeeded by Chan Master Qingbai, who possessed exceptional religious understanding, sharp judgment, and eloquence. He sought to restore declining Buddhist discipline and reconnect religious practice with the highest principles of the Dharma.

The monk Mingtong managed the temple’s accounts, while Rujing organized the collection of donations. Senior officials Lu Guangzu and Wang Shizhen contributed money and building materials. They opened their wealth for the creation of religious merit. Silver was transformed into magnificent halls, fine timber into monastic buildings, pigments into sacred paintings, and metal into Buddhist images.

Before daylight appeared, the bells and stone chimes could already be heard. Even after night had fallen, Buddhist chanting continued.

The surrounding landscape also became part of the religious experience. The cliffs of Dielang formed green walls from which one seemed to hear the sound of the sea. Clear water descended through the ravine below Central Peak, while the stūpa appeared to rise directly from the earth.

Mount She resembled a great Buddhist banner, while its red clouds appeared like a brightly decorated hall. The religious merit created there was so extensive that neither ink nor language could fully describe it. I therefore carve these words upon enduring stone.

Interpretation

Reconstruction through a network of power.

Earlier Qixia inscriptions centered on founders, sacred images, miracles, and royal patronage. Chen Wenzhu instead explains how an ancient institution survived through repeated reconstruction.

Xingshan organized the revival; Qingbai continued monastic leadership; Zhenjie and Fahui restored teaching; Mingtong managed finances; Rujing coordinated donations; and Lu Guangzu and Wang Shizhen supplied money, materials, prestige, and political connections. Reconstruction therefore required spiritual authority, administration, fundraising, property management, craftsmanship, and elite support.

By connecting this late-Ming work to Ming Sengshao, Fadu, and the Hongwu Emperor, the inscription gave the new buildings historical legitimacy. It presents restoration not as a new beginning, but as the recovery of a sacred tradition extending across more than a thousand years.

Historical Background

The late-Ming revival of Qixia.

Written in 1591, the inscription records Qixia Temple’s recovery after long periods of decline. The early Ming state had recognized and supported the monastery, but after the capital moved from Nanjing to Beijing, Qixia no longer enjoyed the same direct court attention.

During the Jiajing and Wanli eras, monks including Xingshan, Qingbai, Fahui, and Zhenjie worked with officials, writers, administrators, and donors to recover resources, reconstruct halls, restore images, regulate the community, and revive Buddhist teaching. Chen Wenzhu’s text commemorates this broader rebuilding of Qixia as a functioning religious, educational, economic, and social institution.

English Translation

Those who seek a jewel bright enough to illuminate a carriage must travel to the place where such jewels are found. Those who desire jade worth several cities must open the valley in which it lies. No one has ever harvested fine silk without first planting mulberry trees. How, then, could anyone receive religious blessings without first creating their causes?

For this reason, charitable giving stands at the beginning of the Six Perfections, while miserliness is among the most serious bonds that restrain the mind. When the task is to adorn the land of the Buddha, how could one continue to cling to wealth?

Ancient records tell how the Elder Anāthapiṇḍada covered the ground with gold to establish a Buddhist monastery. Other patrons commissioned sacred images and received honor and prosperity in later lives. These accounts can be examined in earlier writings and are supported by transmitted tradition. Yet some Confucian scholars, unable to understand consequences extending beyond one lifetime, dismiss them as impossible. Is this not regrettable?

Qixia Temple at Mount She stands in one of the celebrated regions south of the Yangtze and within the beautiful surroundings of the imperial city. It overlooks the great river and is encircled by mountains. A thousand Buddha images occupy its rocky chambers, while its peaks appear to have flown into place or risen suddenly from the earth. It is both an important Buddhist sanctuary and an excellent place from which to view the surrounding landscape.

The temple was first established during the Southern Qi and Liang periods and had survived for more than one thousand years. During the Hongwu reign, the court restored its honored name and granted more than 1,300 mu of mountain and agricultural land for the support of its monks. It was placed alongside the major monasteries of Tianjie and Linggu.

After the Chenghua reign, however, the temple gradually declined. By the beginning of the Jiajing reign, it had nearly become a ruin.

Fortunately, a place becomes spiritually powerful through the people who inhabit it, and great works emerge when conditions are suitable. Accomplished monks cleared the thorns and practiced meditation there, while scholars and officials gathered amid the overgrown grounds. The former abbot Xingshan began restoring the broken institution, although the work had only just started.

When I previously served in the Ministry of Rites, I formed a connection with the White Deer area of the mountain. I later continued to support the temple. When I returned to Mount She after taking higher office in Nanjing, I saw that its Buddhist halls once again shone brilliantly. Its sacred images radiated like an auspicious moon. The ancient appearance of the temple had been unexpectedly transformed by new construction.

I learned that the present abbot, Qingbai, maintained strict monastic discipline and pure religious conduct. When he taught the precepts, even the mountain spirits seemed to listen respectfully; when he preached the Dharma, the four groups of Buddhist followers returned to him. The lecturer Zhenjie also expounded the broad Mahāyāna teachings. Together, these monks contributed to the revival of the monastery.

Consequently, donations gathered like clouds and money flowed like a spring. People competed to contribute, and the work was completed with remarkable speed.

Yet when the project is examined from beginning to end, the contribution of Ni Min was the greatest. The five-bay Hall of the Heavenly Kings was constructed entirely through his support.

Ni came from a prominent family of the Yunjian region and was known in Nanjing as a charitable man. Although he possessed considerable private wealth, his mind was devoted to pure Buddhist deeds. By establishing a foundation in this sacred place, he followed the example left by the recluse Ming Sengshao. His connection with Qixia Temple was especially deep, and he devoted himself both to its construction and to its decoration.

Banners and canopies appeared high in the air, while the surrounding corridors became quiet and solemn. The Hall of the Heavenly Kings rose majestically toward the clouds. The sacred images seemed to gather the brilliance of many suns. When the great bell sounded, its voice penetrated the hidden world; when the painted decoration was completed, its light appeared to rise into the heavens.

The expenditure approached two thousand units of gold-value. Ni Min’s commitment was sincere, and his achievement deserves to endure.

The Buddha taught that causes and consequences follow each other like a shadow following a body. Amitābha guides beings toward salvation, while the Four Heavenly Kings supervise the continents of the world. How could the reward for a virtuous act fail to appear? Through this merit, enlightenment is no longer remote, and rebirth in heaven need not be feared as something unattainable.

Nevertheless, human life is limited, and worldly wealth has no permanent reality. Nine-tenths of the restoration of this mountain monastery has been completed, but one-tenth remains. When a mountain is almost finished, how could one refuse to add the final basket of earth?

Ni should therefore strengthen his resolve and complete this virtuous undertaking. The work should be brought to full perfection so that it possesses both a proper beginning and a proper conclusion.

To establish an auspicious undertaking upon an auspicious foundation is to create something that will not perish. To extend merit through a sanctuary of merit cannot be regarded as wasted effort. By planting a beneficial cause that may endure through immeasurable ages, one opens blessings for all living beings.

The light of Buddhism will shine again through this work. Although I am now old, having lived to see its completion, I can still compose these words in praise of Ni Min.

Interpretation

Who rebuilt Qixia without direct imperial command?

The inscription presents restoration as the work of a social network. Qingbai and other monks supplied religious leadership and project management; officials and writers supplied connections and public legitimacy; Ni Min and other lay patrons supplied money; and the inscription converted private donation into lasting honor.

Lu’s opening argument makes religious merit a fundraising system: blessings require causes, just as silk requires mulberry trees. Architecture then becomes visible evidence of revival. Halls, corridors, statues, bells, banners, and painted decoration demonstrate that Qixia had regained the resources and organization of a major monastery.

By linking Ni Min with Ming Sengshao and recalling Hongwu patronage, Lu represents the late-Ming project as the recovery of an ancient, legitimate tradition rather than the creation of a new institution.

Historical Background

A patronage network completed the revival.

Lu Guangzu wrote the inscription in the ninth lunar month of 1592, after retiring from high office. Qixia had received recognition and land under the early Ming, but declined after the Chenghua period and was close to ruin by the early Jiajing reign.

Xingshan began the large-scale restoration in the sixteenth century; Qingbai continued it with the Buddhist lecturer Zhenjie and a wider network of officials, scholars, and donors. The immediate purpose of Lu’s record was to commemorate Ni Min, the principal donor who independently financed the five-bay Hall of the Heavenly Kings, and to encourage completion of the remaining work.

English Translation

The summoned recluse Ming Sengshao of the Southern Qi supported Chan Master Fadu, who resided at Mount She. During the Taishi era, Ming converted his residence into Qixia Temple. From the Tang dynasty onward, successive courts repeatedly honored and maintained it.

Huiguang, a monk from She County, had inherited the robe and bowl of a revered master. When scholar-officials came to She, they made Huiguang their host and teacher. He later traveled to Nanjing to study the Buddhist canon and built quarters at Qixia Temple.

Wang Yin, a learned recluse from She County, was a friend of Huiguang. Wang once climbed a pagoda at Qiantang and saw a Song-dynasty copy of the Sutra in Forty-Two Chapters carved upon it. He reflected: “Even in the declining years of the Song, men of standing—though not all of them famous—still worked to preserve an inherited scripture and transmit it imperishably. How can those of us who live in the flourishing Ming claim that we have had no leisure to do the same?”

Qixia Temple had formerly possessed a Prajñā Hall, said to have been established by Chan Master Zhikuang, but it had long fallen into ruin. Wang Yin devised a plan for Huiguang to restore the hall. Inside, they built a platform for expounding scripture, several fathoms across and several feet high. They sought out fine old editions of the scripture and asked distinguished men of letters each to copy one chapter. These chapters were carved on stone around the four sides, following the Song precedent. On the two sides of the Dharma Hall, the principal founders were separately honored; among them were Ming Sengshao and Master Fadu.

In a jiazi year, Huiguang traveled among wealthy patrons, assembled artisans and building materials, selected an auspicious day, and began the work. When the undertaking had been organized, Wang Yin introduced him to Wang Daokun and asked Wang to compose this record.

Wang Daokun said: “I established my reputation through Confucian learning. What do I know of the Buddhist canon? What is this thing called prajñā?” Huiguang replied: “The Way is only one. Confucians often reject the Buddhists—but must the two traditions truly fail to accord?”

“When I lived in She, I heard the scholar-officials discuss the learning of Eastern Yue, and I often found meaning in their words. For distinguishing what is high and low and revealing their differences, nothing is better than the sun. For responding to and understanding all under Heaven, nothing is better than knowledge. Why? Because both depend upon illumination.”

“When Heaven and Earth turn toward darkness, all is obscure. But when the sun rises from the Valley of Dawn and ascends over Fusang, colors become distinct. When it reaches the center of the sky, its light spreads across the four quarters and penetrates even the ninefold depths. Yet once it approaches Mount Yanzi in the west, darkness returns as before. The countless things flourish and decline in regular cycles. The Valley of Dawn does not itself produce the sun, nor does Mount Yanzi destroy it; brightness and darkness, openness and obstruction, follow the sun’s passage.”

“Human knowledge is likewise. When I extend my knowledge to completion and reach the farther shore, that is what is called prajñā. The Sutra in Forty-Two Chapters is the earliest essential teaching brought from the West. Does it not speak especially well of prajñā?”

Alas! The learning of Eastern Yue became famous for its teaching of innate knowledge, and Xiangshan had been its first herald. Xiangshan’s arguments only gained their full force through Eastern Yue; yet, when traced to their beginning, prajñā had already grasped the principle.

Ever since Buddhism entered China, students of the Way have commonly treated it as an enemy, as though fragrant herbs and foul weeds could never be stored in the same vessel. This hostility goes too far. A king occupies the center and possesses the Four Seas; only when outer peoples come as guests and the most distant regions arrive at court does the central realm display its dignity. Were he to close the passes and exclude them, how could he demonstrate the breadth of his rule?

When Wang Daokun first heard this explanation of prajñā, he was moved and set Huiguang’s words in order, hoping to silence those who searched only for faults.

Wang Yin heard this and laughed: “Kuafu chased the sun, yet the sun did not hasten because of him. Lord Lu Yang brandished his spear and the sun seemed to retreat three stations. Human force cannot prevail forever. To suppress Buddhism is wrong; to labor anxiously to defend it is also wrong. The Buddha-sun remains exactly as it is.” Wang Daokun approved and recorded these words as well.

As for the full accounts of expenditure and the names of those who contributed money, these were preserved by the administrators and are not recorded here.

Interpretation

A temple as an institution of knowledge.

The restored hall combined oral teaching, reliable scripture editions, literati calligraphy, stone carving, and the memory of earlier founders. Qixia functioned simultaneously as a school, archive, monument, and ritual site.

Huiguang organized the work, Wang Yin supplied the plan, donors supplied money, artisans supplied labor, prominent writers supplied calligraphy, and Wang Daokun supplied the commemorative argument. The record therefore exposes the social machinery behind religious construction.

Huiguang explains prajñā through concepts familiar to Confucian literati—illumination, knowledge, Eastern Yue learning, and Xiangshan. This does not prove that Buddhism and Confucianism were doctrinally identical. It was a rhetorical language that allowed intellectual cooperation without requiring either tradition to surrender its identity.

Historical Background

Scripture, calligraphy, and patronage.

The record dates the project to a jiazi year. Because Wang Daokun lived from 1525 to 1593, the relevant year is inferred to be 1564, during the Jiajing reign.

After seeing a Song precedent at a Qiantang pagoda, Wang Yin proposed rebuilding Qixia’s ruined Prajñā Hall and carving chapters of the Sutra in Forty-Two Chapters around its lecture platform. Huiguang gathered donors, artisans, materials, scripture editions, and elite calligraphy. The completed project transformed architectural restoration into textual preservation and public teaching.

The inscription is strongest as evidence for Ming-period patronage, institutional memory, and Confucian–Buddhist exchange. Its statements about the hall’s early builder are transmitted traditions rather than secure eyewitness history.

English Translation

Opening

Among the renowned monasteries of Jinling, three are especially celebrated. Niushou is distinguished by its mountain, and Hongji by its waters; but for a place that combines the excellence of both mountain and water, none surpasses Qixia.

The Earlier Revival

During the Jiajing era, Yin Mai, another official whose name remains uncertain in the OCR, and Lu Guangzu arrived together. They admired the mountain’s depth, its flourishing trees, sweet fruit, and steep rocks, and judged it suitable for those cultivating the Way. They therefore invited the Chan master Fahui to reside there. Worthy patrons assisted him; members of the metropolitan elite contributed according to their means, restored ruined structures, and local residents even returned land they had occupied.

The Major Rebuilding

Later, the monk Sengding and a wider circle of donors renewed the work. The court eunuch Ke Zhong, while ill, dreamed that a divine visitor touched him and his long-standing sickness disappeared. The figure identified himself as “a guest from Mount She.” Ke Zhong entered the temple to investigate, found the dream fulfilled, and contributed to rebuilding the Great Hero Hall, the Hall of the Heavenly Kings, and the Sutra Repository, with the Sangharama and Patriarch halls arranged beside them. Old foundations were renewed, ruined structures rebuilt, and the former wilderness became a landscape of brilliant red and green architecture.

Why Buildings Matter

Some might say that the true mind is originally still and that visible forms are only provisional names, so why concern oneself with such construction? The text rejects that conclusion. Mind is not confined by size: it may be present in the vast earth or in a speck of dust. Just as living energy appears through every branch, leaf, and flower, and as streams flow from their sources into rivers and channels, material forms can also carry religious meaning.

One should not mistake lifeless negation for the highest teaching or claim that construction cannot be Buddhist work. Those of lesser understanding cling to form and miss emptiness; others cling to emptiness and reject form. Better is to use form as a vessel for intention, allow reverence to produce insight, and guide the spiritually disoriented toward return.

Jiao Hong’s Personal Connection

After leaving office, I repeatedly rested here. Seeking the traces of the Summoned Recluse Ming Sengshao and the Cavalier Attendant Xu Xuan, I felt free and delighted.

Interpretation

When architecture became Buddhist practice.

Jiao Hong directly answers the objection that buildings and images are empty appearances. Visible form, he argues, can become a vessel for intention, reverence, and awakening. Construction is therefore presented as Buddhist practice rather than decoration.

The rebuilding depended on a network: monks supplied leadership, officials provided protection and legitimacy, donors supplied resources, and a court eunuch connected imperial-service wealth with a local temple. New halls restored not only architecture but Qixia’s visibility as a place of worship, learning, travel, and elite sociability.

By invoking Ming Sengshao and Xu Xuan, Jiao Hong placed the 1606 project within Qixia’s Six Dynasties and Southern Tang past. Patronage became merit, construction became religious service, and the new complex appeared as the recovery of inherited history.

Historical Background

A seven-year rebuilding campaign.

By the middle Ming, Qixia had lost buildings, land, and institutional strength. Its recovery began during the Jiajing era through cooperation among monks such as Fahui and Xingshan, officials including Lu Guangzu, and local donors.

The late-Wanli campaign completed in 1606 restored or built the mountain gate, Hall of the Heavenly Kings, Great Hero Hall, Patriarch Hall, Sangharama Hall, Sutra Repository, reception halls, and related structures. Monastic leadership, official protection, elite donations, and court-connected patronage were converted into a functioning religious complex.

Jiao Hong, a Nanjing scholar and former Hanlin official, repeatedly stayed at Qixia after leaving office. His inscription made the rebuilding a public historical document linking the restored temple to its earlier founders and writers.

Record of the Reconstruction of Qixia Temple

《重修棲霞寺記》 · 祝世禄

Source distinction: this inscription must not be confused with Zhu Shilu’s shorter 《修棲霞寺法堂短引》. The sentence “兼山水之胜者，莫如栖霞” belongs to Jiao Hong’s 《栖霞寺修造记》, not to this text.

English Translation Summary

Qixia Temple stood on the southern side of Mount She, surrounded by mountains, streams, caves, Buddhist buildings, and ancient religious monuments. Although the temple possessed a long history, many of its structures and cliff-carved images had deteriorated.

Beginning in 1600, the monk Sengding, known as Master Sankong, worked with the monk Mingtong to organize a major restoration. The work continued for seven years and was completed in 1606.

The reconstruction involved more than repairing temple halls. Damaged Buddhist images at the Thousand Buddha Cliff were restored, old niches were repaired, and new religious spaces were created. Monks directed the religious project, while craftsmen supplied the technical knowledge and physical labor required to repair the stone monuments.

Among these craftsmen was the stoneworker Wang Shou. Later accounts based on the inscription describe him as repairing older images, creating or designing additional niches, and becoming closely associated with what was later called the Stoneworker Hall.

The reconstruction therefore revived both the functioning monastery and the ancient Buddhist landscape surrounding it.

Translation note: This is a readable website summary, not a complete line-by-line translation. A definitive translation should be made directly from the scanned text on printed pages 85–88.

Interpretation

The craftsmen who preserved the sacred landscape.

The seven-year project joined new construction with the conservation of older statues and niches. Qixia’s revival therefore meant both creating Buddhist monuments and repairing inherited ones.

The appearance of the stoneworker Wang Shou changes the historical perspective. Patrons supplied resources, monks organized the project, but craftsmen physically reconstructed the monuments. Repairing a damaged image renewed its sacred presence and allowed worship to continue.

The project preserved older carvings while adding new work, producing the layered landscape visible at Qixia today. Its long duration reveals sustained fundraising, planning, skilled labor, materials, and religious management rather than the achievement of a single heroic founder.

Historical Background

Seven years at the Thousand Buddha Cliff.

The inscription commemorates a restoration lasting from the gengzi year, 1600, to the bingwu year, 1606. Master Sankong Sengding and Mingtong organized work on temple buildings, cliff-carved images, and stone niches.

This campaign belonged to the broader late-Ming revival of Qixia, but Zhu Shilu’s record is especially valuable because it directs attention toward physical conservation and skilled labor. Officials and writers helped attract support, monks managed the religious program, donors financed it, and stoneworkers repaired the monuments themselves.

Stele to the Venerable Master Jiran

《寂然上人碑》

A record of how Qixia Temple turned monastic space, food, labor, and authority into civilian relief during war.

Verified Chinese Passage

民国二十六年七月，卢沟事起，烽火弥漫，旋及沪京。载道流亡，惨不忍睹。上人用大本、志开两法师之建议与襄助，设佛教难民收容所于本寺。老弱妇孺获救者二万三千余人，日供两餐，时逾四月。道途宁静，始遣之归，真盛德也。事变之后……上人留守栖霞，苦极艰深，困行忍迈，铁肩负厄，处之怡然。

The surviving monument is damaged. Only the passage supported by published reports is transcribed here; missing characters are not silently reconstructed.

English Translation

In July 1937, war spread from North China toward Shanghai and Nanjing. Refugees crowded the roads. Acting on the proposal and assistance of the monks Daben and Zhikai, Jiran opened a Buddhist refugee shelter at Qixia Temple.

The stele states that more than 23,000 elderly people, children, women, and other vulnerable civilians received protection. The temple supplied two meals each day for more than four months and sent people home only when the roads became safer.

Jiran remained at Qixia after the occupation and carried responsibility for the temple community and the refugees. The inscription presents his monastic discipline through public action.

Primary Evidence

Read the monument through its damage.

The rubbing preserves the complete monument visually. A final epigraphic transcription should be checked against the rubbing and the published edition in Nanjing Qixiashan zhen shi lu, vol. 2, p. 129.

Historical Background

A monastery outside the Safety Zone.

The stele was erected in 1940, one year after Jiran’s death. It records Qixia Temple’s response to the 1937–1938 occupation of Nanjing.

Although the temple stood outside the central Nanjing Safety Zone, it possessed halls, food stores, agricultural land, monastic personnel, and social connections. These resources allowed it to shelter more than 23,000 people, provide two daily meals, and continue relief work for over four months.

Interpretation

Buddhism became emergency infrastructure.

Qixia’s buildings became shelter, stored grain became meals, monks became organizers, and religious networks became channels for assistance. Compassion appears here as institutional practice.

The stele is commemorative rather than neutral: it turns a complex relief operation into a moral biography of Jiran. Its claims should therefore be compared with letters, photographs, temple records, and administrative documents.

The damaged monument records a second history. The reported erasure of Chu Minyi’s name shows how postwar politics reshaped the physical survival of wartime memory.
